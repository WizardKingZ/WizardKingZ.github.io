The short version

Overview

Sports investing is the business of putting institutional and private capital into professional sports properties: control and minority stakes in teams, stakes in leagues and their media companies, loans to teams and leagues, and the stadiums and districts around them. A team in a closed North American league is a scarce franchise whose value rests on a share of pooled national media money, local revenue it controls and player costs fixed by collective bargaining. European football clubs earn comparable revenue in an open system of promotion and relegation, with higher wage ratios and thinner margins (see sections 3 and 4).

Seven things to know

  1. Team values are at records, and rising multiples did most of the lifting. Forbes puts the average NFL team at US$9.5bn in 2026, up 34% from US$7.1bn, at 13.4x revenue (Forbes); Sportico's 2026 average is US$9.34bn (Sportico). From 2021 to 2025 the average NFL value rose 104% while league revenue rose 74% (Forbes). See section 3.
  2. Control deals clear above the published lists. The Seahawks sale, completed Sep 3, 2026, set the NFL record at US$9.612bn, 14.6x revenue and 43% above Forbes's prior-year value (Wikipedia, secondary; Forbes). The Lakers majority sale at US$12.5bn, announced Aug 12, 2026 and awaiting NBA approval, is the highest price for a US team (CBS Sports, secondary). See section 8.
  3. National media money is the main value driver. The NFL distributed US$14.5bn of national revenue for the 2025 season (Sportico, secondary), and national money made up 62% of NFL team revenue in 2024 (Sportico, secondary). The NBA's 11-year US$76bn package began in 2025-26 (Sportcal, secondary), and the NFL took 10% of ESPN in a deal that closed about Jan 31, 2026 (NBC News, secondary). See sections 4 and 7.
  4. Institutional money enters as capped, passive minority capital. The NFL caps all funds together at 10% of a team (Front Office Sports, secondary), while MLB raised its single-firm cap to 20% from 15% in summer 2026 (Front Office Sports, secondary). NFL funds get neither governance rights nor preferred equity (Akin Gump). See section 6.
  5. Sports capital is consolidating into large platforms. KKR bought Arctos, which manages about US$15bn, for US$1.4bn plus an earnout of up to US$550mm; the deal was announced in Feb 2026 and closed in May 2026 (KKR; KKR). Apollo provided US$2.6bn of debt and equity to the Yankees while the Steinbrenner family kept control (Fortune, secondary). See sections 9 and 10.
  6. Return evidence rests on appraised values, and cash yields are thin. Ares, which raises sports capital and is therefore an interested party, finds that team values compounded at about 13% a year from 2007 to 2024 with a correlation of −0.01 to global equities (Ares). Operating income equals about 1.8% of the 2025 Forbes average value in the NFL and 2.1% in the NBA (author's arithmetic from Forbes), so investors earn mainly through appreciation at sale. See sections 13 and 14.
  7. Losses start with counterparties and owner leverage, not with the teams. Diamond Sports, the largest regional sports network operator, filed for Chapter 11 on Mar 14, 2023, and its successor, Main Street Sports Group, was reported to have missed rights payments in Jan 2026 (Wikipedia, secondary; SportsPro, secondary). In Europe, Eagle Football reportedly defaulted on Ares's mezzanine loans in Oct 2025, and Lyon passed to Michele Kang (The Esk, secondary; Inside World Football, secondary). See sections 12 and 17.

The next dated test is labor: MLB's collective bargaining agreement (CBA) expires at 11:59 p.m. ET on Dec 1, 2026, and owners have proposed a salary cap (Sportico, secondary; see sections 16 and 21). Exhibit 2 collects the figures the rest of the primer relies on.

3

Industry definition and size

Market & Value

North American team equity alone is worth well over half a trillion US dollars on the published lists, and the NFL accounts for roughly half of it (see 3.2). This section defines the market, sizes it by league, traces how values got there and explains why the two main valuation series rarely agree.

3.1 What counts as sports investing

Sports investing here means institutional and private capital committed to professional sports properties: control and minority equity in teams, stakes in leagues and their commercial companies, debt to teams and leagues, and the venues and districts built around them. Media companies, betting operators and sports technology matter only through the revenue they pay to teams and leagues. College sports appear as context because the House v. NCAA settlement now allows direct revenue sharing of up to US$20.5mm per school in 2025-26 (Akerman, secondary).

3.2 Size: team values by league

Forbes puts the average NFL team at US$9.5bn, up 34% from US$7.1bn, and the 32 teams together at more than US$300bn (Forbes). Adding the latest Forbes lists for the NBA, MLB, NHL and MLS gives a North American total of about US$643bn, a figure that mixes edition dates from Oct 2025 to Sep 2026 (author's arithmetic). Revenue multiples follow the same order as values: the NFL, NBA and WNBA trade above 12x revenue, while MLB and European football sit at 7.0x or below.

3.4 Segmentation

The market splits three ways, and each split maps to a different set of rules and buyers.

  • By league: the NFL alone is worth more than the NBA, MLB, NHL and MLS combined on Forbes figures (Exhibit 4).
  • By stake type: control stakes, individual minority stakes, passive fund minority stakes, league-level commercial stakes such as CVC's in LaLiga and Ligue 1, and structured debt or preferred equity (see sections 6, 9 and 10).
  • By investor type: billionaire control buyers, sports-focused funds, credit managers, sovereign wealth funds, pensions and family offices (see section 5).

3.5 Where sources disagree

Forbes and Sportico usually agree on league averages within 10% but can differ by more than 20% on a single club, and transaction prices often land above both. The gaps come from method. Sportico includes related real estate and businesses in its NBA and MLS figures, putting all 30 NBA teams at about US$165bn against Forbes's "just over" US$160bn (Sportico; Forbes). A sale price also reflects control rights, deal structure and timing, which a list estimate cannot observe.

4

How it works: where team value comes from

Market & Value

A North American team's value rests on a share of pooled national media money that it neither negotiates nor competes for, plus local revenue it controls, minus a player cost fixed as a share of revenue by collective bargaining. Exhibit 7 maps the flows; Exhibit 8 compares the four largest leagues.

4.1 National media and revenue sharing

National revenue is the base of the NFL model. The league distributed US$14.5bn across 32 teams for the 2025 season, more than US$450mm per team before league assessments (Sportico, secondary). That pool exceeded the central revenue of all NBA, MLB and NHL teams combined (Bleacher Report, secondary). In 2024 national distributions made up 62% of NFL team revenue, and only the Cowboys earned more than half their revenue locally (Sportico, secondary).

The money comes mainly from 11-year NFL media deals running through 2033, commonly cited at about US$110bn, although Sportico cites US$125bn including later additions (TheWrap, secondary; Sportico, secondary). The NBA's 11-year US$76bn package with Disney, NBCUniversal and Amazon began in 2025-26 (Sportcal, secondary). Section 7 covers the deals in detail.

4.2 Local revenue: tickets, premium seating and local media

Local revenue separates rich teams from average ones. NFL ticketing, including premium seating, brought in US$4.1bn in 2024, or 19% of team revenue, led by the 49ers at US$176mm; concessions, parking and events added US$1.55bn (Sportico, secondary). Local revenue matters more in baseball, where the Dodgers became the first MLB team to reach US$1bn of revenue in 2024 (Sportico, secondary).

Local television, once the second pillar, is breaking down. Diamond Sports filed for Chapter 11 on Mar 14, 2023 and emerged as Main Street Sports Group, which was then reported to have missed rights payments in Jan 2026 (Wikipedia, secondary; SportsPro, secondary). As a result, MLB produces local broadcasts for 15 teams in 2026 (SportsPro, secondary). Section 7.2 covers the collapse.

4.3 Sponsorship and betting partnerships

Sponsorship is the fastest-growing local line. NFL team sponsorship reached US$2.49bn in the 2024 season, up 6%, with financial services contributing US$250mm (Sportico, secondary). MLB team sponsorship reached US$2.05bn in 2025, up 68% since 2022, helped by jersey patches that 28 of 30 teams now sell (Forbes, secondary).

Betting reaches teams mainly through sponsorship and league data deals rather than as a separate revenue line. Early deals were small: in 2018-19 the NFL earned US$62mm from three operators and the NBA US$49.83mm from five (Sportcal, secondary). Current league-wide betting totals were not found. The NFL's 2020 collective bargaining agreement (CBA) counts revenue from legal gambling in stadiums toward the players' "media kicker" (Wikipedia, secondary).

4.4 Real estate and venues

Venues and the land around them give owners revenue that sits outside the national pool. The Braves paired a US$622mm ballpark with The Battery Atlanta, a mixed-use development budgeted at US$452mm (Wikipedia, secondary). The Chiefs' proposed Kansas project totals US$4bn across stadium, district and headquarters, split 60% public and 40% team (KCUR, secondary). The valuation lists capture part of this value: Sportico's US$23bn total for all 30 MLS clubs includes real estate and related businesses (MLSsoccer.com). Section 11 covers stadium finance.

4.5 The cost side: players, caps and CBAs

CBAs fix the player share of revenue, which caps the largest cost and makes team margins predictable. NFL players receive 48% of all revenue, rising to 48.8% through the media kicker, under a CBA that runs through the 2030 season (Wikipedia, secondary). NBA players receive 51% of basketball related income (BRI), enforced by a 10% salary escrow; players forfeited more than US$480mm in 2024-25 (Sportico, secondary). MLB has no salary cap, and its CBA expires at 11:59 p.m. ET on Dec 1, 2026; owners have proposed a US$245mm cap and a US$171mm floor (Sportico, secondary).

With cost tied to revenue, operating income scales with the league. Forbes estimates average operating income at US$127mm for NFL teams, US$113mm for NBA teams and US$74mm for NHL teams (Forbes). European football lacks this link: Premier League wages reached £4.4bn, or 65% of £6.8bn revenue, in 2024/25 (Deloitte).

5

Buyers and demand drivers

Market & Value

Demand comes from a widening buyer base chasing a fixed supply of teams, and league rule changes since 2019 have let institutional money join billionaires at the table. Prices set records in 2026 because both conditions held at once.

Who buys and why

  • Individual control buyers still set the price, now usually backed by institutions. The Seahawks group led by Vinod Khosla had 12 co-owners including Sixth Street, Carlyle and Dynasty Equity (Wikipedia, secondary).
  • Sports-focused funds buy passive minority stakes. Arctos, with about US$15bn of assets under management (AUM), is the only firm approved for multi-team ownership in all five major US leagues; KKR bought it for US$1.4bn plus an earnout of up to US$550mm (KKR).
  • Credit and multi-strategy managers mix equity, preferred equity and debt. Apollo provided US$2.6bn of debt and equity to the Yankees while the Steinbrenner family kept control (Fortune, secondary).
  • Sovereign wealth funds enter mostly where rules allow. QIA paid US$200mm for 5% of Monumental Sports & Entertainment, while NFL rules limit sovereigns to 7.5% of an approved fund (Front Office Sports, secondary).
  • Pensions invest through funds. CalPERS committed US$775mm to a Sixth Street sports fund in Nov 2025 (Bloomberg, secondary).
  • Family offices and wealthy families take minority stakes directly. Julia Koch and family bought 10% of the Giants at US$10.3bn (CNBC, secondary).

Why prices rose

Scarcity is the base driver: closed leagues add teams rarely, and expansion fee estimates for the NBA run to US$7–10bn per team, above the league's US$5.4bn Forbes average (Flashscore, secondary). Media growth adds income on top. Rule changes widened the buyer pool, and the US tax code lets buyers amortize about 100% of the purchase price over 15 years, a regime the 2025 One Big Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA) left intact after a House proposal to cap it at 50% was excluded from the final law (SABR, secondary; Withers). Higher rates work the other way, raising the cost of acquisition debt.

League ownership rules

Rules, Media & Deals

North American leagues admit institutional money only as capped, passive minority capital, while European football leaves fund stakes uncapped and instead polices who controls a club and how it spends. Exhibit 10 sets out the rules by league.

Every major league now allows funds, and the NFL remains the most restrictive. MLB moved first in 2019, the NBA and NHL had rules in force by 2021, and NFL owners voted 31–1 to admit approved private equity funds on Aug 27, 2024 (Akin Gump; Front Office Sports, secondary).

NFL

The NFL caps institutional money at 10% of a team, whether one fund or several hold it. A single fund may own 3–10% of a team's common equity, and stakes from several firms together cannot exceed 10%, which matches the Patriots deal (Sixth Street 3% plus a 5% individual investor) (Front Office Sports, secondary). A fund must hold for at least six years and may own stakes in up to six teams (Private Equity Wire, secondary). It also needs US$2bn of committed capital, cannot put more than 20% of its assets into one team and gets neither governance rights nor preferred equity (Akin Gump). Sovereign wealth funds cannot invest directly, but they can own up to 7.5% of an approved fund (Front Office Sports, secondary).

Only a handful of firms qualify, and only four have invested. The approved list is Arctos, Ares and Sixth Street plus a consortium of Blackstone, Carlyle, CVC, Dynasty Equity and Ludis, from which Blackstone and CVC have since left (Private Equity Wire, secondary). Deals so far cover the Bills, Chargers, Dolphins, Patriots and Browns, with Arctos's 10% of the Falcons at US$10.6bn awaiting an owner vote expected in Oct 2026 (Front Office Sports, secondary; Hoodline, secondary). Controlling owners must hold at least 30% (Private Equity Wire, secondary), and debt is capped separately (see section 10).

NBA, MLB, NHL and MLS

The other leagues allow 20% per fund and about 30% in aggregate. The NBA set its rules in 2021: 20% per fund, five teams per fund, no more than 30% of any team held by funds and no governance rights (Front Office Sports, secondary). An NBA fund needs at least US$750mm of assets and may not put more than 25% of them into one team, and the league allows sovereign exceptions (Akin Gump). The Qatar Investment Authority used that route to buy 5% of Monumental Sports & Entertainment for US$200mm in 2023 (Front Office Sports, secondary). Funds reach 20 of 30 NBA teams in some form, but fewer than 10 teams have direct fund investors (Front Office Sports, secondary).

The NHL and MLS mirror these limits with lower entry tickets. NHL funds may hold non-voting stakes in up to five teams with a US$20mm minimum (PE Insights, secondary). MLS limits funds to four clubs and requires US$500mm of committed capital (Akin Gump). The NBA, NHL, MLB and MLS do not ban sovereign wealth funds (Front Office Sports, secondary).

Rule of thumb (author's): outside the NFL, funds can own up to about 30% of a team in aggregate; inside it, 10%. Funds own economics, not votes.

6.2 Europe

European football has no fund caps but three overlapping control regimes: a statutory regulator in England, UEFA's multi-club limits and Germany's members' majority. League-level private equity sits outside these club rules (see section 12.4).

England has moved from league self-regulation to a statutory owner test. The Independent Football Regulator gained powers over current owners in the top five tiers on Dec 12, 2025, including fines, censure and forced sales (Sky Sports, secondary). From May 2026 all new owners, directors and senior executives need the regulator's approval (same source). Premier League clubs also adopted a Squad Cost Ratio that limits squad costs to 85% of football revenue and net player-trading profit from 2026/27, with a 30% multi-year allowance above it subject to a levy (Premier League). UEFA's equivalent threshold is 70%, and the clubs rejected "top-to-bottom anchoring," which would have worked as a de facto salary cap (same source).

UEFA's multi-club rule decides eligibility on a single date. Two clubs under common "decisive influence" cannot enter the same UEFA competition, and from 2025/26 the test date is Mar 1 before the season instead of Jun 3 (Peterka Partners). UEFA moved Crystal Palace down to the Conference League on Jul 11, 2025 because Eagle Football controlled both Palace and Lyon on Mar 1, and the Court of Arbitration for Sport dismissed its appeal on Aug 11, 2025 (Sky Sports, secondary). Owners avoid the rule with blind trusts or restructurings, as Girona/Man City and Man United/Nice did in 2024/25 (Peterka Partners).

Germany's 50+1 rule survived its antitrust review. In Aug 2026 the Bundeskartellamt found the members'-control rule permissible if applied consistently. It asked for stronger protections for already exempt clubs such as Leverkusen and Wolfsburg and set no deadline (Bundeskartellamt).

6.3 Changes in 2025–2026 and pending

Rules are loosening in North America and tightening in England. MLB and the NBA widened access for funds in 2026 while the NFL held its line:

  • Nov 2025–May 2026: England added the Squad Cost Ratio and the regulator's owner powers and approval test (see section 6.2).
  • Summer 2026: MLB raised its single-firm cap to 20% from 15%, set a 15% control-owner minimum and barred a fund from owning more than the control owner; about 10 MLB teams have fund investors (Front Office Sports, secondary).
  • Sep 18, 2026: the NBA let a firm invest in a team alongside its executives, provided the firm holds no interest in another team, a change that cleared a path for the Lakers sale (Bloomberg via Yahoo Finance, secondary).
  • Sep 24, 2026: the NFL kept its 10% cap and did not expect to raise it at the Oct 2026 meeting (Front Office Sports, secondary).

Four decisions are pending. NFL owners are expected to vote on the Arctos–Falcons stake in Oct 2026 (Hoodline, secondary). The NBA Board of Governors has yet to approve the Lakers sale (Bloomberg via Yahoo Finance, secondary). English clubs apply for regulator licences between Nov 1, 2026 and Feb 26, 2027 (Skadden; Lewis Silkin gives Nov 2). Multi-club owners must restructure before UEFA's Mar 1, 2027 test date for 2027/28 competitions (Peterka Partners).

7

Media rights

Rules, Media & Deals

National media contracts are the largest and most predictable revenue line for North American teams, and their renewals set the pace of team values. The NFL's 2025 national revenue exceeded the central revenue of all NBA, MLB and NHL teams combined (Bleacher Report citing Sportico, secondary; see section 4). Three shifts now shape the market: national deals are repricing upward, local cable rights have collapsed and streamers and equity swaps are displacing traditional buyers.

7.1 National deals

The NFL and NBA have locked in long, rising contracts; the NHL and MLB face renewals before 2029. The NBA's 11-year, US$76bn package with Disney, NBCUniversal and Amazon began in 2025-26, and Disney's new fee of about US$2.6bn a year is 73% above its previous one (Sportcal, secondary). MLB took the opposite approach with three-year deals through 2028 that add about US$800mm a year from ESPN, NBC/Peacock and Netflix (Axios, secondary).

7.2 The local-TV collapse

The failure of the largest regional sports network operator removed a guaranteed revenue line for dozens of teams. Diamond Sports had borrowed nearly US$9bn to buy 21 Fox regional networks and filed for Chapter 11 on Mar 14, 2023 after missing a US$140mm interest payment (NewscastStudio, secondary; Wikipedia, secondary). It emerged on Jan 2, 2025 as Main Street Sports Group but missed rights payments in Jan 2026, and nine MLB teams terminated their contracts (MLB Trade Rumors, secondary). On Apr 6, 2026 Main Street announced it would cease operations after the 2025-26 NBA regular season and NHL first round, leaving 13 NBA and seven NHL teams without a carrier (NewscastStudio, secondary).

Leagues have stepped in, but at lower guaranteed fees. MLB produces local broadcasts for 15 teams in 2026, and ESPN distributes MLB.TV, including in-market games (SportsPro, secondary). That model pays teams less guaranteed revenue than the old cable contracts (MLB Trade Rumors, secondary). Local rights now feed into labor talks: MLB owners have said they would share local TV rights if players accept a salary cap ahead of the Dec 1, 2026 CBA expiry (Fox News, secondary; see section 16).

7.3 Streaming and equity-for-rights

The NFL took equity in its partner instead of cash alone. The NFL–ESPN deal closed about Jan 31, 2026: ESPN acquired NFL Network, cable and satellite distribution of RedZone and NFL Fantasy, and the NFL received 10% of ESPN (NBC News, secondary). Neither party disclosed a price or a value for the stake (Wikipedia, secondary).

Streamers are now core buyers rather than add-ons. Amazon pays the NBA US$1.8bn a year (Sportcal, secondary). Netflix extended its NFL deal by four years in May 2026 and expanded it to five games (Deadline, secondary). Apple holds Formula 1's exclusive US rights from 2026 for about US$140mm a year (Axios, secondary). Apple's MLS deal shows the risk on the other side: the original US$2.5bn, 10-year contract was restructured, and MLS ended Season Pass to put all matches on Apple TV from 2026 (Front Office Sports, secondary).

7.4 Europe's rights market

Europe's domestic rights have flattened outside England and Spain, and France shows the downside. Deloitte expects Big Five revenue growth to slow in 2025/26 and 2026/27, with a possible plateau or decline in some markets (Deloitte).

  • Premier League: domestic rights rose to £6.7bn over four seasons from 2025/26, against £5bn for the prior three (SportsPro, secondary). International rights of about £2.1bn a year, up 27%, take the cycle to £12.25bn (SportsPro, secondary). Club broadcast revenue still grew only 2% to £3.4bn in 2024/25 (Deloitte).
  • UEFA: UEFA's gross revenue threshold for its club competitions is €4.4bn for 2026/27, of which it will distribute €3.317bn to clubs (UEFA Circular 48/2026). From 2027, Relevent becomes the global commercial partner, and UK rights rise to £565mm a season (about £2.26bn over four seasons, author's arithmetic), up from £455mm (SportsPro, secondary).
  • LaLiga: the 2027–2032 domestic deal is worth €6.135bn, up 9% a season, split almost evenly between Telefónica and DAZN (Inside World Football, secondary).
  • Bundesliga and Serie A: the Bundesliga's 2025–29 deal rose 2% to €4.48bn, and Serie A's €4.5bn deal through 2028/29 was below its predecessor (SportsPro, secondary; Sportico, secondary).
  • Ligue 1: DAZN walked away from its five-year deal, and the league's own streaming service, Ligue 1+, had more than one million subscribers at €14.99 a month (SportsPro, secondary). The league expects to distribute about €142mm of media revenue in 2025/26, against about €500mm from broadcasters previously (same source). Ligue 1 club revenue fell 15% to €2.2bn in 2024/25 (Deloitte).

The French case matters for investors because CVC owns 13% of Ligue 1's media company, bought for €1.5bn in 2022, and has reportedly cut the stake's valuation (Inside World Football, secondary; OneFootball, secondary; see section 12.4). Media rights lift values when buyers compete, and they cut them when a single buyer exits.

Rules, Media & Deals

Control prices for the scarcest teams reset in 2025–2026, and minority stakes in NFL teams have cleared at or near published valuations rather than at a visible discount. The Los Angeles Lakers agreed a majority sale to Josh Kushner and Bob Iger at US$12.5bn on Aug 12, 2026, the highest price for a US team, with NBA approval still pending (Forbes, secondary). The Seattle Seahawks sold for US$9.612bn, an NFL record, and the deal completed on Sep 3, 2026 (Wikipedia, secondary).

8.4 How stakes are priced

Control premium. Control buyers pay above the published lists when a scarce team comes to market. The Seahawks cleared at 14.6x revenue against the 13.4x NFL average (Forbes via Yahoo), and the Kushner–Iger Lakers price sits 25% above Forbes's Oct 2025 Lakers value, which matched Walter's US$10bn purchase (Forbes; author's arithmetic).

Minority discount. No systematic dataset on minority discounts was found, and the available deals show little discount. The Giants stake at US$10.3bn compares with Forbes's 2025 Giants value of US$10.1bn, and the Patriots stake at US$9bn matches Forbes's 2025 Patriots value (Sportcal, secondary; Forbes). A Miami Marlins stake of about 15% sold at US$1.55bn in 2025, above Sportico's US$1.45bn valuation of the team (Sportico, secondary). Fund stakes nonetheless carry limits that would normally widen a discount: in the NFL they have no governance rights, cannot use preferred equity (Akin Gump) and must be held for at least six years (Private Equity Wire, secondary).

Rule of thumb (author's): where the buyer pool is capped and few stakes come to market, scarcity offsets the usual minority discount, so a passive stake prices close to the latest list value.

Staged and blended deals. Tranches and blended prices let one headline value hide several prices. Arctos is buying 10% of the Falcons in two tranches over 18 months at a blended US$10.6bn (Hoodline, secondary). The Raiders sold 25% and 3.5% at US$9.9bn and US$11bn in the same season (Forbes via Yahoo). Sellers also keep residual stakes, as the Buss family did with about 15% of the Lakers in 2025 (CBS Sports, secondary), or grant options: Fenway Sports Group sold about 38% of Liverpool to 1892 Holdings, led by Amit Bhatia, with an option for the buyer to take control within 12 months (JOE, secondary).

Financing limits. Debt caps push buyers toward large equity groups. An NFL club may carry US$700mm of debt, and a new buyer up to US$1.5bn (section 10), so the Seahawks group assembled 12 co-owners, including Sixth Street, Carlyle and Dynasty Equity (Wikipedia, secondary). Section 10 covers debt limits.

9

Sports funds and institutional investors

Capital & Venues

A small group of managers holds most institutional capital in sports, and its strategies fall into four types: passive multi-team minority funds, flexible credit and hybrid capital, league-level commercial stakes and permanent-capital platforms. KKR's purchase of Arctos in 2026 folded the most active minority-stake specialist into a manager with US$759bn of AUM (Dakota, secondary).

9.1 Fund strategies and structures

  • League-sanctioned minority funds hold passive, multi-team stakes. The NFL requires an approved fund to have US$2bn of committed capital and limits any one team to 20% of fund assets; the NBA requires at least US$750mm of assets and a 25% concentration limit (Akin Gump).
  • Credit and hybrid capital funds teams without diluting control. Apollo provided US$2.6bn of debt and equity to the New York Yankees in Aug 2026, and the Steinbrenner family kept full control (Fortune, secondary). Ares put US$500mm of preferred equity into BlueCo, Chelsea's owner, in Sep 2023 (The Esk, secondary).
  • Revenue participations buy a share of a club's income rather than its equity. Sixth Street paid €360mm for a share of non-sporting revenue at Real Madrid's Bernabéu (Malay Mail, secondary) and bought 25% of Barcelona's LaLiga TV rights for 25 years (Barca Blaugranes, secondary).
  • League-level stakes buy a share of a whole competition's media income. CVC holds commercial stakes in LaLiga and Ligue 1, among others (SportsPro, secondary); section 12 covers these deals.
  • Permanent and semi-liquid vehicles widen the investor base. Apollo Sports Capital draws on Athene's balance sheet as permanent capital (Dakota, secondary). Ares plans a semi-liquid fund with quarterly redemptions for individual investors (WealthManagement, secondary).
  • Newer managers are raising smaller, focused funds. Otro Capital closed its first fund at US$1.2bn in Feb 2026 (Business Wire). Mark Cuban's Harbinger Sports Partners closed at US$450mm with a 7–10 year exit horizon, and Ariel Investments reached a US$250mm first close for a women's sports fund (Dakota, secondary).

9.3 Liquidity and exits

Exits remain the asset class's weak point, and the KKR–Arctos deal is the clearest sign that managers are building their own liquidity. KKR agreed to buy Arctos, which manages about US$15bn, for US$1.4bn of initial consideration plus an earnout of up to US$550mm; the deal was announced on Feb 4, 2026 and closed in May 2026 (KKR/Business Wire; KKR close release). Arctos was the only firm approved for multi-team ownership in all five major US leagues (KKR/Business Wire).

  • Control sales are the main exit for minority funds. Dyal HomeCourt bought into the Phoenix Suns at a US$1.55bn valuation in Jul 2021 and exited at US$4bn when Mat Ishbia bought control, a 158% gain over about 18 months (Sportico, secondary).
  • Dedicated liquidity capital is growing. Arctos entered 2025 with US$11.3bn in sports-related funds plus a US$2.7bn liquidity strategy (Sportico, secondary), and its Keystone fund closed at US$6.2bn in Jun 2026 to provide GP financing (PitchBook).
  • Exit uncertainty slowed NFL adoption. Unanswered questions about how funds would exit NFL stakes slowed the first wave of deals (Front Office Sports, secondary), and only four approved firms had invested as of Sep 2026 (Front Office Sports, secondary).
  • Price gaps can stall sales. Otro's group holds 24% of the Alpine F1 team, and talks to sell it to Mercedes failed over valuation in Jun 2026 (RACER, secondary).
  • League-level platforms are heading to public markets. CVC plans an IPO or sale of Global Sport Group within about three years (SportsPro, secondary).

Fund performance remains private: coverage of the KKR deal disclosed no Arctos IRR or distributions (Sportico, secondary). Section 14 reviews the return evidence.

9.4 Sovereign and pension capital

Sovereign funds reach US teams mostly through other managers' vehicles, while Gulf funds own clubs directly in Europe. The NFL bars direct sovereign investment but lets a sovereign fund own up to 7.5% of an approved private equity vehicle (Front Office Sports, secondary). The NBA allows sovereign equity of up to 20%, and MLS is open to sovereign investment (Morgan Lewis).

  • QIA invests as a passive minority holder in the US. It paid US$200mm for 5% of Monumental, owner of the Wizards and Capitals, in 2023 (Front Office Sports, secondary), and added shares in 2025 at a 78% higher enterprise value (section 8.2).
  • PIF is shifting toward returns. PIF's 2026–2030 strategy emphasizes financial returns, and it has sold 70% of Al-Hilal while keeping about 85% of Newcastle (Golf Channel, secondary). It plans to stop funding LIV Golf after the 2026 season, and Newcastle is discussing a minority stake sale (SportsPro, secondary).
  • US public pensions invest as fund LPs. CalPERS committed US$775mm to a Sixth Street sports fund in Nov 2025 (Bloomberg, secondary), and Oregon PERF committed US$150mm to Arctos's third fund in Apr 2026 (Dakota, secondary). Arctos's LP base spans pensions, endowments, insurers, family offices and wealth platforms (Business Wire).
  • Canadian pensions have owned teams directly. Ontario Teachers' bought into Maple Leaf Sports & Entertainment for C$180mm in 1994 and sold its 79.53% to Bell and Rogers for C$1.32bn in 2011 (Benefits Canada, secondary). Rogers took full ownership on Oct 1, 2026 by buying the remaining 25% for a reported 4.35bn (currency not stated in the source) (Daily Faceoff, secondary).
  • Family offices often enter through consortia. The Eduardo Saverin family office joined 1892 Holdings at Liverpool (JOE, secondary), and the Khosla, Griffith and Deeter families bought about 6% of the San Francisco 49ers at US$8.6bn (Front Office Sports, secondary).

10.3 European club finance

European clubs borrow on their own credit. Premier League clubs held £3.6bn of net debt in 2024/25 and lost £948mm before tax (Deloitte). Manchester United reported net debt of £749mm at Sep 30, 2025, plus about £447mm of transfer payables (The Esk, secondary). Four kinds of borrowing dominate:

  • Media-rights notes. Barcelona's notes carry a first-priority claim on its LaLiga media revenue and a BBB rating from Morningstar DBRS, and they priced 202bps over the benchmark against 240bps on the prior issue (The Esk, secondary).
  • Long-dated stadium debt. Tottenham's £851.7mm of borrowings are more than 90% fixed at an average 3.07% with an average maturity of 17.6 years (Tottenham Hotspur). Real Madrid borrowed €575mm over 30 years at no more than 2.5% for the Bernabéu in 2019 (SportsPro, secondary).
  • Owner-level acquisition debt. RedBird refinanced AC Milan through Comvest Credit Partners in Jan 2026, repaying the Elliott vendor loan from its 2022 purchase; the amount was not disclosed (AC Milan). Oaktree, which had financed Inter's former owner Suning, took control of the club in May 2024 (Bloomberg, secondary).
  • Rescue and hybrid capital. Eagle Football was reported to have defaulted on Ares's mezzanine loans in Oct 2025 and later entered UK administration; Michele Kang then took 87.78% of Lyon in Jun 2026 (The Esk, secondary; Inside World Football, secondary; see section 17).

Relegation is the main credit risk. KBRA rates Olympique Lyonnais's €320mm Groupama Stadium financing BBB+ and calls relegation the biggest credit variable in stadium deals (KBRA). Closed North American leagues carry no such risk.

12

European football and global sports capital

Capital & Venues

European football generates large revenue but little profit, and US and Gulf capital now controls a large share of its top clubs. Promotion and relegation, high wage ratios and multi-club structures add risk that closed US leagues do not carry, so some investors buy league-level media stakes or Formula 1 instead.

12.1 Economics of European football

Revenue keeps rising while losses persist. The European football market reached €40.2bn in 2024/25, up 6%, and the Big Five leagues earned €21.6bn of it (Deloitte). Premier League clubs earned £6.8bn, but wages took 65% of revenue and only eight clubs made an operating profit, down from 13 (Deloitte). Ligue 1 revenue fell 15% to €2.2bn after its media deal collapsed (Deloitte; see section 7). Across all top divisions, UEFA counts €28.6bn of club revenue and €1.1bn of pre-tax losses in 2024, with 53% of clubs profitable before tax (Football Benchmark). Deloitte expects Big Five growth to slow in 2025/26–2026/27 (Deloitte).

Promotion swings club values. The 2026 Championship play-off final was worth at least £205mm of extra revenue over three seasons to the winner, and about £365mm if it survives its first Premier League season (Deloitte).

12.2 US and Gulf ownership

US investors now control a large share of Europe's top clubs. About 40% of Big Five clubs are controlled by American investors in 2025-26, according to PitchBook (PitchBook). UEFA counted 29 controlling-stake sales in 2025, and North American investors made one-third of all top-flight takeovers (SportsPro, secondary, citing UEFA). Eleven Premier League clubs had US owners in 2025/26 (Sports Illustrated, secondary). Recent deals show the range:

  • Liverpool. FSG sold about 38% to 1892 Holdings, led by Amit Bhatia with Jeff Bezos and the Saverin family office, at a reported US$7bn valuation, with an option to take control within 12 months (JOE, secondary).
  • Chelsea. Clearlake, with 61.5%, was reported to be negotiating to buy out Todd Boehly and Mark Walter at a £5bn club valuation; no deal had been agreed by Aug 18, 2026 (Insider Sport, secondary).
  • Everton and Crystal Palace. The Friedkin Group bought 98.8% of Everton in Dec 2024 (Sky Sports, secondary), and Woody Johnson bought Eagle Football's 43% of Palace for a reported £190mm in Jul 2025 (Sports Mole, secondary).

Gulf sovereign funds are shifting toward returns. PIF's 2026–2030 strategy emphasizes financial returns (Golf Channel, secondary). It owns about 85% of Newcastle and is discussing a minority sale and a securitization of commercial revenue (SportsPro, secondary). PIF has put more than US$5.3bn into LIV Golf since 2021 and plans to stop funding it after the 2026 season (SportsPro, secondary), and it sold 70% of Al-Hilal (Golf Channel, secondary). QIA takes passive stakes in US teams instead: it bought 5% of Monumental Sports at a US$4.05bn enterprise value in 2023 and more alongside Arctos at US$7.2bn in 2025 (Sportico, secondary). The NFL bars direct sovereign ownership (see section 6).

Multi-club groups now own about half of Big Five clubs. About 48% of Big Five clubs belonged to multi-club operators in 2025-26, up from 41.7% a year earlier (PitchBook). UEFA counts 345 clubs that are part of multi-club ownership networks, on FY2024 data (Football Benchmark); a summary of the same report gives 122 (SportsPro, secondary), possibly for a narrower scope such as top divisions only. Ares's semi-liquid fund offers quarterly redemptions on assets that rarely trade (WealthManagement, secondary).

The main risks are concentration risks. A team depends on one league's media contracts, one labor agreement and one venue, and section 17 shows each failing. European clubs also carry relegation risk, which KBRA names as the biggest credit variable in stadium financings (KBRA).

Regulation, tax and labor

Economics & Risk

The US tax treatment that supports buyer economics survived its first serious challenge in 2025, while the active regulatory changes sit in English football, European competition law, college sports and MLB labor. Sections 6 and 12 cover ownership and multi-club rules.

Tax: the 15-year write-off survived. Since 2004, buyers can amortize about 100% of a franchise purchase price over 15 years under Section 197, replacing the earlier rule that limited player-contract write-offs to 50% over five years (SABR, secondary). On a purchase of about US$700mm, such as the Astros in 2011, that creates about US$46.7mm of deductions a year (SABR, secondary). The House draft of the One Big Beautiful Bill Act would have cut the amortizable amount to 50% of basis (Proskauer), but the final law signed Jul 4, 2025 excluded the limit (Withers) and left carried interest unchanged (Kirkland).

Antitrust: courts are testing league control. A jury awarded US$4.7bn (US$14.1bn trebled) against the NFL over Sunday Ticket in Jun 2024, and the judge threw out the award in Aug 2024 (NPR, secondary). The Ninth Circuit heard the appeal on Mar 9, 2026 and has not ruled (Courthouse News, secondary). In Europe, the EU Court of Justice held on Dec 21, 2023 that FIFA and UEFA prior-authorization rules breached competition law (Bird & Bird). Germany's Bundeskartellamt closed its review of the 50+1 rule in Aug 2026, finding it permissible if applied consistently (Bundeskartellamt).

English football now has a statutory regulator. The Independent Football Regulator gained powers over current owners on Dec 12, 2025, including fines and forced sales, and new owners have needed its approval since May 2026 (Sky Sports, secondary). The Premier League adds a Squad Cost Ratio of 85% from 2026/27 (Premier League) and a front-of-shirt betting sponsor ban that left 11 clubs facing about £80mm of lost revenue (SportsPro, secondary). An independent commission was reported in late Sep 2026 to have found against Manchester City on almost all of the 115 financial charges; City may appeal, and sanctions have not been set (NBC News, secondary). UEFA fined 14 clubs under its €60mm three-year loss limit and 70% squad cost rule, led by Strasbourg at €25mm (Inside World Football, secondary).

College sports now pay athletes. The House v. NCAA settlement allows revenue sharing of up to US$20.5mm per school in 2025-26 (CBS Sports, secondary). The Senate passed the Protect College Sports Act 77–22 on Sep 28, 2026, which would ban super leagues and cap conferences at 20 schools (CBS Sports, secondary).

Labor: MLB is the near-term risk. Owners have proposed baseball's first salary cap since 1994, at US$245mm with a US$171mm floor, and the minimum salary would rise from US$780,000 to US$1mm (Sportico, secondary). The interim union head calls a cap non-negotiable, and owners have offered to share local TV rights if one is adopted (Fox News, secondary). The last lockout began Dec 1, 2021 and lasted three months and eight days (Sportico, secondary), and Yankee Stadium bonds hold reserves to cover debt service through a 2027 lockout (Sportico, secondary).

Trends and outlook

Players & Outlook

Six trends are reshaping how institutional capital meets sports, and together they point to more capital, more structure and more regulation at once.

  • Leagues are loosening institutional limits at different speeds. MLB raised its single-firm cap to 20% in summer 2026, and the NBA now lets a firm co-invest with its executives (Front Office Sports, secondary; Bloomberg via Yahoo, secondary). The NFL is holding at 10% (Front Office Sports, secondary).
  • Sports managers are being absorbed into large platforms. KKR bought Arctos, Apollo draws on Athene's permanent capital and CVC plans an IPO or sale of its Global Sport Group within about three years (see section 9; SportsPro, secondary).
  • Media money is shifting from cable bundles to national streaming deals. The NFL's early renegotiations stalled in Aug 2026, leaving its 2029-30 opt-out as the next reset (Sports Media Watch, secondary). Locally, MLB produces broadcasts for 15 teams in 2026 after the regional network collapse (SportsPro, secondary).
  • Private credit is moving into sports at both ends of the risk range. Barcelona's media notes were more than 200% oversubscribed, while Eagle Football and 777 Partners show the losses at the leveraged end (see sections 10 and 17; The Esk, secondary).
  • European regulation is tightening on owners and costs. The UK regulator opens licensing in Nov 2026, the Premier League's 85% squad cost ratio starts in 2026/27, and UEFA fined 14 clubs under its financial rules in mid-2026 (Skadden; Premier League; Inside World Football, secondary).
  • Women's sports are repricing fastest from a small base. Forbes put the average WNBA team at US$414mm in 2026, up 52%, and the new WNBA labor deal lifts the salary cap to US$7mm from US$1.5mm (Forbes; SportsPro, secondary).

Outlook. Growth looks uneven by region. Deloitte projects slowing Big Five growth through 2026/27, with a possible plateau in some markets (Deloitte). In North America the main near-term risk to cash flow is labor: owners have proposed MLB's first salary cap since 1994 ahead of the Dec 1, 2026 expiry (Sportico, secondary).

Glossary

Appendices
50+1
German rule that keeps a majority of club votes with members (section 6).
Aggregate fund cap
Limit on the share of one team that all funds together may own; 10% in the NFL, 30% in the NBA, NHL and MLS (section 6).
Apron
NBA payroll threshold above the tax line that triggers roster restrictions.
Basketball related income (BRI)
The NBA revenue pool from which players receive 51% (section 4).
Blind trust
Structure that removes an owner's decisive influence over a club to meet UEFA multi-club rules (section 6).
Collective bargaining agreement (CBA)
League-union contract that sets the player share, cap and labor calendar (section 16).
Control premium
Amount a control buyer pays above a list or minority price (section 8).
Decisive influence
UEFA test of common control between clubs, applied on Mar 1 (section 6).
Earnout
Deferred price paid if targets are met, such as up to US$550mm in KKR–Arctos (section 9).
Escrow
Share of player salaries withheld to keep pay at the agreed revenue split (section 4).
Expansion fee
Price a new team pays to join a closed league (section 8).
GP financing
Lending to fund managers rather than teams, as in Arctos's Keystone fund (section 9).
Hockey related revenue (HRR)
The NHL revenue pool used to set player pay.
Independent Football Regulator (IFR)
English statutory regulator that approves owners and licenses clubs (section 16).
League credit facility
Central borrowing vehicle that lends to teams against national media revenue (section 10).
Media kicker
NFL CBA term that lifts the player share to 48.8% as media revenue grows (section 4).
Media-rights notes
Bonds secured on a club's or league's media income (section 10).
Mezzanine
Subordinated, high-coupon debt; Ares's loans to Eagle Football ran at 16–19.4% (section 10).
Minority discount
Price reduction for a stake without control; recent NFL stakes show little (section 8).
Opt-out
Right to end a media contract early; most NFL partners have one after 2029-30 (section 7).
PILOT bonds
Stadium bonds repaid from payments in lieu of taxes (section 10).
Preferred equity
Equity ranking ahead of common, with a fixed return; barred for NFL funds (section 6).
Regional sports network (RSN)
Cable channel holding local team rights; the model collapsed with Diamond Sports (section 7).
Revenue participation
Purchase of a share of a club's income rather than its equity (section 9).
Secondaries
Purchases of existing fund or team stakes from current holders (section 9).
Securitization
Bonds backed by a pool of contracted revenue, such as the MLB Trust (section 10).
Semi-liquid fund
Fund offering periodic redemptions on illiquid assets (section 14).
Sovereign wealth fund
State investment fund; the NFL caps one at 7.5% of an approved fund (section 6).
STAR bonds
Kansas sales-tax revenue bonds proposed for a Chiefs stadium (section 11).
Squad Cost Ratio
Premier League limit on squad costs as a share of revenue (section 6).
