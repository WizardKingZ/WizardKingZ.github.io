This trip was not an escape. I arrived in Japan in December 2025 with more open questions than answers, and with a plan that was really a hypothesis: that a country where solitude is not loneliness, where restraint is a form of strength, and where beauty often lives in what is left unsaid, might be a good place to reset my orientation. I did not need answers. I wanted clarity of mind, steadiness of body, and a little quiet.

The route followed the Tōkaidō, the old "Eastern Sea Road" between the shogun's city of Edo and the emperor's city of Kyoto, and on to Osaka. Each city turned out to have something to teach, so I have written it up that way: Tokyo for independence, Kyoto for restraint, and Osaka for ease.

## Tokyo: Motion, Scale, and Independence

Fri 19–Tue 23 Dec · Tokyo, Mt Fuji, Hakone
{: .dayline}

### Edo, Then Tokyo

Tokyo is a young capital by Japanese standards. It began as Edo, a castle town that Ōta Dōkan fortified in 1457. Tokugawa Ieyasu made it his base in 1590, became shogun in 1603, and for the next two and a half centuries Edo was where Japan was actually governed, even though the emperor stayed in Kyoto. When the shogunate fell in 1868, Edo was renamed Tokyo, the "eastern capital," and the emperor moved into the castle.

What is left of that castle is now the Imperial Palace. Most of it is closed to visitors, but its moats and walls run through the business district, and the Tatsumi Yagura, a two-story corner turret, is one of only three that survive. The office towers behind it make the point about scale better than any museum could.
![A white two-story Edo Castle corner turret on a stone wall above the moat, with office towers behind](/assets/images/japan-2025/tatsumi-yagura.jpg)
*The Tatsumi Yagura, one of three surviving turrets of Edo Castle, on the Imperial Palace moat.*
{: .caption}
![An old iron grille set in stone, with the palace moat blurred behind it](/assets/images/japan-2025/palace-moat-grille.jpg)
*Ironwork on a bridge over the palace moat.*
{: .caption}

Across the moat, Tokyo Station faces the palace on purpose. Tatsuno Kingo's red-brick building opened in 1914, lost its domes to the air raids of 1945, made do with octagonal roofs for decades, and was restored to its original form in 2012.
![The red-brick Marunouchi building of Tokyo Station, with glass towers behind it](/assets/images/japan-2025/tokyo-station.jpg)
*Tokyo Station, restored in 2012 to its 1914 design.*
{: .caption}

The road system Ieyasu built began a few blocks away. In 1603 he had a wooden bridge put across the river at Nihonbashi, and the next year it became the starting point of the five great roads out of Edo, the Tōkaidō to Kyoto among them. Distances in Japan are still measured from a marker set in its road. The current stone bridge opened in 1911, with bronze kirin on its lamp posts. Their wings are said to stand for flight, departures from this point to the rest of the country. In 1963, in the rush to prepare for the Tokyo Olympics, an expressway was built directly over the bridge; it is now being moved underground, with the overhead section due to come down by 2040. One bridge south, a stone post carved 京橋, "the bridge toward the capital," survives from the Kyōbashi bridge of 1875, though the river under it was filled in long ago.
![A bronze winged kirin on the Nihonbashi bridge, under the expressway](/assets/images/japan-2025/nihonbashi-kirin.jpg)
*A winged kirin on Nihonbashi, under the expressway built over it in 1963.*
{: .caption}
![A stone bridge post carved with the characters for Kyōbashi](/assets/images/japan-2025/kyobashi-pillar.jpg)
*A post from the 1875 Kyōbashi bridge, the next bridge down the Tōkaidō.*

### Temples, Shrines, and the Crossing

My first full day ran from the oldest of the city to the newest. Sensō-ji in Asakusa is traditionally dated to 628, when two fishermen found a statue of Kannon in their net. Its inner gate, the Hōzōmon, hangs a red lantern marked 小舟町, for the neighborhood that donated it, between two great black-and-gold lanterns. Behind the main hall, koi drift under a stone bridge, and the noise of the shopping street disappears within a few steps.
![A huge red paper lantern hanging in the vermilion Hōzōmon gate of Sensō-ji, flanked by two black-and-gold lanterns](/assets/images/japan-2025/hozomon.jpg)
*The Hōzōmon at Sensō-ji. The red lantern names the Kobunachō neighborhood that gave it.*
{: .caption}
![Koi swimming in a dark pond under an arched stone bridge](/assets/images/japan-2025/sensoji-koi.jpg)
*Koi in the garden at Sensō-ji.*
{: .caption}

Meiji Jingu, by contrast, is barely a century old. It was dedicated in 1920 to Emperor Meiji, who opened Japan to the West, and to his empress. Its forest feels ancient, but it was planted: about 100,000 trees were donated from across Japan and beyond, and they have grown into a 70-hectare wood in the middle of the city. The great torii on the approach, about 12 meters tall, is made of Taiwanese cypress more than 1,500 years old, and its crossbeam carries the imperial chrysanthemum. Along the path, sake brewers have stacked their barrels as offerings; across from them, so do winemakers from Burgundy, a nod to the emperor's taste for French wine.

Twenty minutes later I was looking down on the Shibuya crossing, where up to about 3,000 people cross on a single light change. One moment I was in quiet greenery; the next I was watching thousands of people flow past one another in near silence. It was crowded but not chaotic. Everyone had somewhere to be, and no one was in anyone else's way. That was when I understood something about Tokyo: here, independence is not defiance. It is normal.
![The Shibuya scramble crossing from above, full of people, surrounded by billboards](/assets/images/japan-2025/shibuya-crossing.jpg)
*The Shibuya crossing from above, mid-scramble.*
{: .caption}

I ate alone without explanation and walked without a destination, and the city absorbed me. Golden Gai in Shinjuku and the arcades of Akihabara were Tokyo at its most playful. Kabukichō, under its red neon arch, is named for a kabuki theater planned there after the war that was never built. At the foot of Tokyo Tower, Zōjō-ji, the Tokugawa family temple where six of the shoguns are buried, sits in front of a 1958 steel tower painted orange and white for air safety. The city glows without trying to impress, and there is comfort in that.

### Mt Fuji and Hakone

Leaving the city made Tokyo feel even sharper in hindsight. Mt Fuji, at 3,776 meters, last erupted in 1707, and UNESCO lists it as a cultural site rather than a natural one, as a "sacred place and source of artistic inspiration." It did not demand attention. It sat in its own cloud, immovable and indifferent, and put scale back into perspective. My problems shrank without disappearing, which seemed like the right balance.
![Mt Fuji wrapped in heavy cloud, with snow on its upper slopes and houses in the foreground](/assets/images/japan-2025/fuji-clouds.jpg)
*Fuji, mostly in its own weather.*
{: .caption}

In the afternoon I crossed into Hakone, the volcanic hot-spring district where the old Tōkaidō climbed over the mountains and the shogunate kept a checkpoint. Steam still vents from the hillsides, and a cable car hauls visitors up slopes the old travelers walked. I came back to Tokyo tired, but steadier.
![Steam venting from a forested, cloud-covered mountainside in Hakone](/assets/images/japan-2025/hakone-steam.jpg)
*Steam on the hillside in Hakone.*
{: .caption}
![The track of the Hakone cable car running steeply downhill between station roofs](/assets/images/japan-2025/hakone-cable-car.jpg)
*The Hakone cable car line, looking down the mountain.*
{: .caption}

### Odaiba: The Future's Waiting Room

My last Tokyo day was spent on reclaimed land in the bay. Odaiba takes its name from the cannon batteries the shogunate built here in 1853 and 1854, after Commodore Perry's warships arrived to force Japan open; two of them survive. The island now feels like the future's waiting room: wide, quiet, and slightly unreal, with a replica of the Statue of Liberty facing the Rainbow Bridge and, until 2026, a life-size Unicorn Gundam standing guard over a shopping mall.
![A replica of the Statue of Liberty in Odaiba, with the Rainbow Bridge across the bay](/assets/images/japan-2025/odaiba-liberty.jpg)
*Odaiba's Statue of Liberty, with the Rainbow Bridge behind.*
{: .caption}
![The white Unicorn Gundam statue in front of DiverCity Tokyo Plaza](/assets/images/japan-2025/odaiba-gundam.jpg)
*The Unicorn Gundam at DiverCity. Update, 2026: it was taken down at the end of August.*
{: .caption}

At teamLab Planets, visitors take off their shoes and walk, partly through water, into rooms of digital art. It was impressive, but also strangely calming. The technology did not overwhelm; it invited a kind of quiet curiosity. By the end of that day I felt complete with Tokyo. I did not need more.

What stayed with me was how quiet the city actually is, how normal it feels to be alone, how efficiency can feel humane, and how the absence of eye contact comes with an absence of judgment. Tokyo taught me that self-containment is a kind of strength.

## Kyoto and Nara: Stillness, History, and Inner Space

Wed 24–Fri 26 Dec · Kyoto, Nara, Arashiyama
{: .dayline}

On the morning I left, I ate oysters at the Tsukiji outer market, then took the Shinkansen down the same corridor the Tōkaidō once followed. The line opened in 1964, the world's first high-speed railway, and covers in a little over two hours what took travelers on foot about two weeks.
![A large raw oyster held up at a stall in the Tsukiji outer market](/assets/images/japan-2025/tsukiji-oyster.jpg)
*Breakfast at Tsukiji.*
{: .caption}

### Kyoto

Kyoto was the emperor's city, as Heian-kyō, from 794 until 1869, and it arrived softly. It does not announce itself; it reveals itself slowly, in wooden buildings, narrower streets, and warmer light. I went first to Fushimi Inari, the head shrine of Inari, the god of rice and prosperity, founded in 711. Its paths run under thousands of vermilion torii, each given by a business or a family and inscribed with the donor's name and the date. Read closely, the mountain is a ledger of thanks for good years.
![A tunnel of vermilion torii gates at Fushimi Inari, inscribed in black](/assets/images/japan-2025/fushimi-inari.jpg)
*The torii at Fushimi Inari, each inscribed with its donor.*
{: .caption}

At dusk I walked through Gion, past Yasaka Shrine and down Hanamikoji, and along the Shirakawa canal. Time stretched. Nothing was rushed, and even my thoughts slowed down. The light caught the five-story pagoda of Hōkan-ji, the "Yasaka pagoda," above the tiled roofs.
![The five-story Yasaka pagoda above tiled rooftops at sunset](/assets/images/japan-2025/yasaka-pagoda.jpg)
*The Yasaka pagoda at dusk.*
{: .caption}
![A tray of wagyu slices, vegetables in a hot pot, rice, and dipping sauces](/assets/images/japan-2025/kyoto-wagyu.jpg)
*Wagyu for dinner in Kyoto.*
{: .caption}

### Nara

On Christmas Day I went to Nara, Japan's capital before Kyoto, from 710 to 784, in the rain. Nara feels ancient in a way that is not performative. The path to Kasuga Taisha, a shrine founded in 768, is lined with some 3,000 stone and bronze lanterns, most of them furred with moss. The shrine's gods are said to have arrived riding a white deer, which is why Nara's deer, now about 1,465 of them, are treated as sacred messengers and protected as a national Natural Monument. They move freely, unconcerned with tourists or time.
![Moss-covered stone lanterns lining a path at Kasuga Taisha](/assets/images/japan-2025/kasuga-lanterns.jpg)
*Stone lanterns on the approach to Kasuga Taisha.*
{: .caption}
![Two deer on a wet stone path in front of the Great South Gate of Tōdai-ji](/assets/images/japan-2025/nara-deer.jpg)
*Deer at the Great South Gate of Tōdai-ji. The plaque reads 大華厳寺, "Great Kegon Temple."*
{: .caption}

Tōdai-ji was built by Emperor Shōmu as the head of a network of state temples, and its Great Buddha was dedicated in 752. The figure is about 15 meters tall. The hall around it, rebuilt in 1709, is about a third smaller than the original and still one of the largest wooden buildings in the world. The Buddha does not intimidate; it steadies. Beside it stands Kōmokuten, one of the Four Heavenly Kings who guard the directions, holding a brush and scroll to record what he sees. I felt small, but not insignificant. That distinction matters.
![The Great Buddha Hall of Tōdai-ji in rain and mist, across a lawn](/assets/images/japan-2025/daibutsuden.jpg)
*The Daibutsuden, rebuilt in 1709.*
{: .caption}

### Arashiyama

On my last morning in Kyoto I went west to Arashiyama, early enough to have the bamboo grove almost to myself, and rode out along the Hozu River gorge. At Hozukyo station, a crowd of ceramic tanuki waits on the platform. In folklore the tanuki is a shape-shifting trickster; in ceramics it is a cheerful figure of good luck, round-bellied and holding a sake flask.
![A path running through a tall bamboo grove in morning light](/assets/images/japan-2025/arashiyama-bamboo.jpg)

{: .caption}

I noticed the sound of footsteps on stone, how the light changed in the afternoon, how little signage there was, and the restraint in architecture and color. Kyoto reminded me that depth does not need volume.

## Osaka: Warmth, Appetite, and Release

Fri 26–Sat 27 Dec · Osaka
{: .dayline}

Osaka, once called Naniwa, greeted me differently. The city was louder, looser, and more human. People laughed more openly, food was everywhere, and it was less polished and more alive. After Kyoto's introspection, it felt like permission to re-engage.

Its castle tells the story of how the Tōkaidō's two ends came to be ruled from Edo. Toyotomi Hideyoshi, who unified Japan, began Osaka Castle in 1583. After his death, Tokugawa Ieyasu besieged his heir there in 1614 and 1615 and destroyed the Toyotomi line. The museum inside has a diorama of the last battle in front of a painted screen; on the left, the red-armored troops of Sanada Yukimura, the defender who led the final charge and died in it. The main tower today is a 1931 reinforced-concrete reconstruction, paid for by the people of Osaka. From the top, a gilded shachihoko, a fish with a tiger's head meant to protect the roof from fire, looks out over the moats.

*Osaka Castle's main tower, rebuilt in 1931.*

{: .caption}

Osaka has long called itself the nation's kitchen, and its motto is *kuidaore*, to eat until you drop. In Dōtonbori, the Glico running man, on his billboard in one form or another since 1935, crosses the finish line all night. In Shinsekai, the "New World" district laid out in 1912, a giant tengu mask advertises grilled meat and a shooting gallery promises good luck.
![The Glico running-man billboard in Dōtonbori at night, with a clock reading Dec. 26, 2025](/assets/images/japan-2025/glico.jpg)
*The Glico sign in Dōtonbori, on 26 December 2025.*
{: .caption}

Osaka did not ask me to reflect. It asked me to enjoy. Walking through Dōtonbori on my last night, I realized it was the right ending: I did not leave Japan in silence, I left it with energy. I noticed the warmth in casual exchanges, the way food brings people together, the lack of pretense, and the contrast with Kyoto, and why both matter. Osaka reminded me that life is meant to be tasted, not just understood.

## Rebalanced

The trip moved in an arc. Tokyo taught me independence, Kyoto taught me restraint, and Osaka taught me ease. I did not leave Japan transformed. I left it rebalanced, and that was enough.
