This trip was not an escape. I arrived in Japan in December 2025 with more open questions than answers, and with a plan that was really a hypothesis: that a country where solitude is not loneliness, where restraint is a form of strength, and where beauty often lives in what is left unsaid might be a good place to reset my orientation. I did not need answers. I wanted clarity of mind, steadiness of body, and a little quiet.

The route followed the Tōkaidō, the old "Eastern Sea Road" between the shogun's city of Edo and the emperor's city of Kyoto, and on to the merchants' city of Osaka. Each turned out to have something to teach: Tokyo independence, Kyoto restraint, and Osaka ease. Along the way, the trip also ran backward through Japanese history, from the Tokyo of today to Edo, the courts of Kyoto and Nara, and the wars that decided who would rule. So I have written it up by city, with the history and the food folded in where I met them.

## Kami and Buddha, Emperor and Shogun

Two ideas help with almost everything a visitor sees in Japan.

The first is that Japan has two religions, and most people practice both. Shinto is the older one. It has no founder and no single scripture; it holds that *kami*, gods or spirits, live in the natural world, in mountains, rivers, trees, rocks, even earthquakes and volcanoes. In that sense it resembles other nature religions, such as the old Norse one. Shinto sites are shrines, marked by a torii gate. Buddhism arrived in the 6th century, by tradition in 538 or 552, from India by way of China and the Korean kingdom of Baekje, bringing ideas of impermanence and enlightenment, monks, and scripture. It did not replace Shinto. It layered onto it, and for most of Japanese history the two were practiced together, often on the same grounds. Buddhist sites are temples. The habit continues: the government's religious yearbook counts about 87 million Shinto adherents and 83 million Buddhists in a country of about 125 million, because so many people are counted by both. A common description is that Japanese life begins at the shrine and ends at the temple, which handles most funerals.

Zen, the school of Buddhism best known abroad, came later. The monk Eisai brought Rinzai Zen back from China in 1191, along with a taste for powdered green tea, which he promoted in a book on the health benefits of drinking it. Dōgen founded the Sōtō school after returning in 1227. Zen's emphasis on discipline and direct experience appealed to the warrior class, and it shaped ink painting, rock gardens, and the tea ceremony.

The second idea is the difference between the emperor and the shogun. The imperial house is often called the oldest continuous hereditary monarchy in the world; by legend it descends from the sun goddess Amaterasu, and the current emperor, Naruhito, who acceded in 2019, is counted as the 126th. For most of the last thousand years, though, the emperor reigned while someone else ruled. From the late 12th century, real power lay with the shogun, the military ruler at the head of the samurai. Japan's history is largely the story of how those two roles were divided, fought over, and in 1868 put back together.

### Edo

Tokyo is a young capital by Japanese standards. It began as Edo, a castle town that the warrior Ōta Dōkan fortified in 1457. Ten years later, the Ōnin War broke out in Kyoto and began more than a century of civil war among regional lords, the Sengoku or "Warring States" period. It ended with three unifiers in turn: Oda Nobunaga, Toyotomi Hideyoshi, and Tokugawa Ieyasu. Ieyasu, who had made Edo his base in 1590, won the decisive battle of Sekigahara in 1600 and became shogun in 1603. For the next 265 years, the Edo period, his family ruled from here in peace, with the emperor in Kyoto and foreign contact restricted, from the 1630s, mainly to the Dutch and Chinese at Nagasaki.

What is left of Edo Castle is now the Imperial Palace, next to Tokyo Station. Most of its grounds are closed, but its moats and walls run through the business district, and the Tatsumi Yagura, a two-story corner turret, is one of only three that survive. The office towers behind it make the point about scale. In the late-1980s bubble, the palace's land was said to be worth more than all the real estate in California.

The shogun's roads began a few blocks away. In 1603 Ieyasu had a bridge put across the river at Nihonbashi, and the next year it became the starting point of the five great roads out of Edo, the Tōkaidō to Kyoto among them. Distances in Japan are still measured from a marker in its road. The current stone bridge opened in 1911, with bronze kirin on its lamp posts whose wings are said to stand for journeys out across the country. In 1963, in the rush to prepare for the Tokyo Olympics, an expressway was built directly over it; it is now being moved underground, with the overhead section due to come down by 2040. One bridge south, a stone post carved 京橋, "the bridge toward the capital," survives from the Kyōbashi bridge of 1875.

Edo was also where much of what the world now calls Japanese food took shape, as fast food for a crowded city of working men. Nigiri sushi, a slice of fish pressed onto vinegared rice, is credited to a vendor named Hanaya Yohei in the 1820s. Tempura had arrived earlier with Portuguese traders in Nagasaki and became an Edo street snack. Eel grilled in a sweet soy glaze, *kabayaki*, was another. Near Kanda I had it over rice for lunch, with [karaage](/kitchen/#r-chicken-karaage) on the side.

### Meiji: The Emperor Comes to Edo

In 1853 Commodore Perry's American warships anchored in Edo Bay and demanded that Japan open its ports. The shogunate built cannon batteries, *daiba*, on artificial islands in the bay; two survive, and the district around them is still called Odaiba. The shogunate could not hold off the pressure, and in 1868 a coalition of domains overthrew it in the name of the emperor. The Meiji Restoration renamed Edo as Tokyo, the "eastern capital," moved the teenage emperor into the castle, and set out to industrialize the country within a generation.

Meiji Jingu, dedicated in 1920 to that emperor and his empress, is Shinto's answer to the modern city. It lies between Harajuku and Shinjuku, and its forest feels ancient, but it was planted: about 100,000 trees were donated from across Japan and beyond, and they have grown into a 70-hectare wood. The great torii on the approach, about 12 meters tall, is made of Taiwanese cypress more than 1,500 years old, and its crossbeam carries the imperial chrysanthemum. Along the path, sake brewers have stacked barrels as offerings; across from them, so have winemakers from Burgundy, a nod to the emperor's taste for French wine.

Meiji Jingu was the first shrine of the trip, so it is where I learned how to pray at one. Bow once before passing under the torii, and walk to the side of the path, since the center is said to belong to the kami. At the water basin, the *temizuya*, take the ladle in your right hand and rinse your left hand, then your right; pour a little water into your cupped left hand to rinse your mouth, spit it to the side, and rinse the ladle's handle before putting it back. At the hall, drop a coin in the offering box, ring the bell if there is one, then bow twice, clap twice, pray, and bow once more. At a Buddhist temple the gestures are quieter: a bow, a coin, and palms pressed together, with no clapping.

Meiji changed the menu too. Buddhist teaching had discouraged eating meat for centuries, and in 1872 the emperor ate beef in public to show the country that modern people did. Beef hot pots, the ancestor of sukiyaki and of the [gyūdon](/kitchen/#r-gyudon-with-onsen-egg) beef bowl, spread through the port cities. Western-style dishes, *yōshoku*, followed, among them [curry rice](/kitchen/#r-japanese-beef-curry) and the breaded pork cutlet that became [tonkatsu](/kitchen/#r-adam-liaws-tonkatsu). Chinese cooks in Yokohama's Chinatown brought the noodle soup that became ramen; the first dedicated ramen shop opened in Asakusa in 1910.

Tokyo Station faces the palace on purpose. Tatsuno Kingo's red-brick building opened in 1914, lost its domes to the air raids of 1945, made do with octagonal roofs for decades, and was restored to its original design in 2012.

![The red-brick Marunouchi building of Tokyo Station, with glass towers behind it](/assets/images/japan-2025/tokyo-station.jpg)
*Tokyo Station, restored in 2012 to its 1914 design.*
{: .caption}

After the war, the 1946 "Humanity Declaration" set aside the emperor's divine status, and the 1947 constitution made him "the symbol of the State and of the unity of the people." Shinto and the imperial house remain, as Meiji Jingu shows, but as heritage and ritual rather than as instruments of the state.

### Sensō-ji, Shibuya, and the Tokyo of Today

My first full day ran from the oldest part of the city to the newest. Sensō-ji in Asakusa is traditionally dated to 628, when two fishermen found a statue of Kannon in their net, which makes it older than Edo by eight centuries. Its inner gate, the Hōzōmon, hangs a red lantern marked 小舟町, for the neighborhood that donated it. Behind the main hall, koi drift under a stone bridge, and the noise of the shopping street disappears within a few steps.

That afternoon I was looking down on the Shibuya crossing, where up to about 3,000 people cross on a single light change. One moment I had been in quiet greenery at Meiji Jingu; the next I was watching thousands of people flow past one another in near silence. It was crowded but not chaotic. Everyone had somewhere to be, and no one was in anyone else's way. That was when I understood something about Tokyo: here, independence is not defiance. It is normal.

Tokyo is less one city than a set of them. Shibuya is youth, fashion, and energy; Shinjuku is offices, the metropolitan government, and nightlife. Akihabara's arcades and Shinjuku's alleys were Tokyo at its most playful. Kabukichō, under its red neon arch, is named for a kabuki theater planned there after the war that was never built. Taiyaki, the fish-shaped cake filled with sweet bean paste that I ate on the street nearby, is said to date from 1909, late in the Meiji era. At night the city glows without trying to impress, and there is comfort in that.

The Japan I visited was also in the middle of a change that I follow for work. Sanae Takaichi had become its first woman prime minister two months earlier. A record 42.7 million foreign visitors came in 2025, and on some streets it felt like it. After decades of near-zero interest rates, the Bank of Japan has been raising rates and shrinking its holdings of government bonds, which it had bought until it owned about half the market. ### Mt Fuji and Hakone

Leaving the city made Tokyo feel even sharper in hindsight. Mt Fuji, 3,776 meters high, is an active volcano that last erupted in 1707, and in Shinto it is a kami. The goddess of the mountain is Konohanasakuya-hime, "the princess who makes the trees blossom," often linked with the cherry blossom, and she is worshipped at some 1,300 Sengen shrines, headed by Fujisan Hongū Sengen Taisha at the mountain's foot. Fear of the volcano and reverence for it grew together; worship was partly a way to calm it.

At the fifth station, the road ends beside Komitake Shrine, which honors her older sister, Iwanaga-hime, the goddess of rock. In the old chronicles, the heavenly prince Ninigi was offered both sisters and chose the beautiful Konohanasakuya-hime over the enduring Iwanaga-hime, and so, the story goes, human lives became as brief as blossoms rather than as lasting as stone. It is the Japanese idea of impermanence in a single myth.

![Komitake Shrine at the Mt Fuji fifth station, with a torii, a sacred rope, and guardian lion-dogs, and snow on the ground](/assets/images/japan-2025/komitake-shrine.jpg)
*Komitake Shrine at the fifth station, for Iwanaga-hime, the goddess of rock.*
{: .caption}

Climbing Fuji was historically a pilgrimage rather than a sport. In the Edo period, confraternities called Fuji-kō saved up to send members to the summit for purification, and women were barred from climbing the mountain until 1872. The mountain did not demand attention when I saw it. It sat in its own cloud, immovable and indifferent, and put scale back into perspective. My problems shrank without disappearing, which seemed like the right balance.

![Mt Fuji wrapped in heavy cloud, with snow on its upper slopes and houses in the foreground](/assets/images/japan-2025/fuji-clouds.jpg)
*Fuji, mostly in its own weather.*
{: .caption}

It is also perhaps the most painted mountain in Japan, and the story of how it came to be painted is the story of Japanese painting in brief. Court painters of the Heian period developed *yamato-e*, a native style of bright color and gentle landscapes. Zen monks brought ink painting from China, and the monk Sesshū made it Japanese in the 15th century. The Kanō school painted gold-leaf folding screens for warlords and shoguns. Then, in Edo, woodblock prints, *ukiyo-e*, "pictures of the floating world," put art within reach of merchants and townspeople; printers even used techniques such as *mokume-zuri*, printing the grain of the wood itself for texture. In the Meiji era, the word *nihonga* was coined for painting in these traditions, to tell it apart from the Western-style painting arriving at the same time.

Its most famous image came from an artist who was already old. Katsushika Hokusai was born in Edo in 1760 and took his name from Katsushika, the farming district east of the city where he grew up. He was apprenticed to a woodblock carver as a boy and joined the studio of the print designer Katsukawa Shun'shō at about 18. He is said to have used more than 30 names over his career and to have moved house more than 90 times, and he was in his seventies when the publisher Nishimuraya Yohachi brought out his *Thirty-Six Views of Mount Fuji*. In the afterword to a later Fuji book, he wrote that nothing he drew before 70 was worth noticing, and that by 110 every dot and line would be alive; he died in 1849, at 88, reportedly wishing for a few more years to become a real painter. He signed some of his late work Gakyō Rōjin, "the old man mad about painting."

The first print of the series, *Under the Wave off Kanagawa*, printed around 1830 to 1832, is often called the best-known Japanese image in the world. A wave the size of a cliff curls over three fishing boats, and Fuji sits small and calm in the distance, the one thing in the picture that will still be there afterward. Hokusai printed it with Prussian blue, a pigment newly imported from Europe, and the print later traveled back the other way: Debussy put it on the cover of the score of *La Mer* in 1905. Public-domain impressions are in the [Metropolitan Museum of Art](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/56353) and the [Art Institute of Chicago](https://www.artic.edu/artworks/24645).

### Nara: Where Buddhism Became the State's

Nara was Japan's first permanent capital, from 710 to 784, laid out on the grid of the Chinese capital at Chang'an. It is where Buddhism became an affair of state, and it feels ancient in a way that is not performative. Emperor Shōmu ordered a temple in every province and built Tōdai-ji at the head of them; its Great Buddha was dedicated in 752. The figure is about 15 meters tall, and the hall around it, rebuilt in 1709, is about a third smaller than the original and still among the largest wooden buildings in the world. The Buddha does not intimidate; it steadies. Beside it stands Kōmokuten, one of the Four Heavenly Kings who guard the directions, holding a brush and scroll to record what he sees. I felt small, but not insignificant. That distinction matters.

### Kyoto: The Emperor's City

The court moved to Heian-kyō, modern Kyoto, in 794, and the emperors stayed for more than a thousand years, until 1869. Kyoto arrived softly. It does not announce itself; it reveals itself slowly, in wooden buildings, narrower streets, and warmer light.

I went first to Fushimi Inari, founded in 711 and the head of some 30,000 to 40,000 Inari shrines across Japan. Inari began as the kami of rice, and since rice was once how wealth, taxes, and power were measured, Inari became the kami of business and prosperity as well. That makes it one of the most practical gods in Shinto. The shrine is less a building than a mountain, which suits a religion that places the divine in nature rather than in buildings: its paths climb Mount Inari under thousands of vermilion torii, each given by a business or a family, often in thanks after a wish came true, and inscribed with the donor's name and the date. The vermilion is said to ward off misfortune and, practically, to help protect the wood from decay. The foxes that guard the paths are not gods but Inari's messengers, chosen because foxes hunt the rodents that eat the rice; they often hold a key to the granary, a jewel, or a scroll in their mouths, for access to abundance, stored wealth, and knowledge.

Walking through the gates is an act of gratitude rather than petition, and the long climb turns that into a ritual. As the path rises, the crowds thin, the gates grow older, and the mountain grows quieter; the full walk to the summit and back takes two to three hours, and many visitors turn back early. The idea it expresses is reciprocity: effort comes first, and thanks follow success. It is easy to see why it suited Kyoto's merchants, and why it still suits a country that values patience and long commitment.

### Zen, Tea, and the Kyoto Table

Kyoto is also where Zen became a way of living rather than only a doctrine. The great Zen temples here were patrons of ink painting and garden design, and the tea ceremony grew out of them. Sen no Rikyū, a merchant's son trained in Rinzai Zen, refined it in the 16th century as tea master to Hideyoshi, into the spare, rustic style called *wabi-cha*. The light meal served before tea became *kaiseki*, Japan's formal cuisine, and the temples' vegetarian cooking, *shōjin ryōri*, shaped it too. What reached the ordinary Kyoto table was *obanzai*, home-style dishes served in small portions, many at once, nearly all of them built on [dashi](/kitchen/#r-dashi-stock), the kelp-and-bonito stock that is also the base of [miso soup](/kitchen/#r-miso-soup).

On my last morning I went west to Arashiyama, early enough to have the bamboo grove almost to myself; it runs beside Tenryū-ji, one of the city's great Zen temples. Then I rode out along the Hozu River gorge, where a crowd of ceramic tanuki waits on the platform at Hozukyo station. In folklore the tanuki is a shape-shifting trickster; in ceramics it is a round-bellied figure of good luck.

## Osaka: Warmth, Appetite, and Release

Fri 26–Sat 27 Dec · Osaka · Days 8 and 9
{: .dayline}

Osaka, once called Naniwa, greeted me differently. It was louder, looser, and more human. People laughed more openly, food was everywhere, and it was less polished and more alive. After Kyoto's introspection, it felt like permission to re-engage.

### The Last Battle of the Warring States

Osaka Castle is where the Sengoku period finally ended. Toyotomi Hideyoshi, the second of the three unifiers, began it in 1583. After he died, Tokugawa Ieyasu, already shogun, besieged his heir there in the winter of 1614 and the summer of 1615 and destroyed the Toyotomi line. The defenders' most famous commander, Sanada Yukimura, led a last charge toward Ieyasu's headquarters and was killed. The museum inside the castle tells the story with a diorama in front of a painted folding screen, the kind of gold-ground *byōbu* that warlords commissioned to record their battles; the labels name Sanada Yukimura's troops on the left and those of Matsudaira Tadanao, who fought them, on the right. The best-known screen of the battle, the *Ōsaka Natsu no Jin Zu Byōbu*, was commissioned by Kuroda Nagamasa, a general on the Tokugawa side, and is an Important Cultural Property in the castle's collection.

The main tower today is a 1931 reinforced-concrete reconstruction, paid for by the people of Osaka. From the top, a gilded *shachihoko*, a fish with a tiger's head meant to protect the roof from fire, looks out over the moats.

### The Nation's Kitchen

With peace, Osaka became the commercial capital of Edo Japan. Domains from across the country shipped their tax rice here to be sold, and the city became known as *tenka no daidokoro*, "the nation's kitchen." At the Dōjima Rice Exchange, officially recognized by the shogunate in 1730, merchants traded contracts on future rice deliveries, which is why Dōjima is often called the world's first organized futures market. As someone who works in markets, I liked the thought that futures trading began with Inari's crop.

The city's motto is *kuidaore*, to eat until you drop. In Dōtonbori, the Glico running man, on his billboard in one form or another since 1935, crosses the finish line all night. In Shinsekai, the "New World" district laid out in 1912, a giant tengu mask advertises grilled meat and a shooting gallery promises good luck.

Osaka did not ask me to reflect. It asked me to enjoy. Walking through Dōtonbori on my last night, I realized it was the right ending: I did not leave Japan in silence, I left it with energy. I noticed the warmth in casual exchanges, the way food brings people together, the lack of pretense, and the contrast with Kyoto, and why both matter. Osaka reminded me that life is meant to be tasted, not just understood.

## Rebalanced

The trip moved in an arc. Tokyo taught me independence, Kyoto taught me restraint, and Osaka taught me ease. Underneath all three ran the two ideas I started with: kami in the mountains and the trees, and the Buddhist sense that everything passes, which is why the blossom is loved more than the rock. I did not leave Japan transformed. I left it rebalanced, and that was enough.

## Getting Around

Japan is easy to move around once three things are sorted.

- **An IC card.** Suica (JR East) and PASMO in Tokyo, and ICOCA in the Kansai region around Kyoto and Osaka, are rechargeable cards you tap at the ticket gates of trains, subways, and most buses, and at convenience stores and vending machines. The cards largely work across each other's networks, though not for every journey that crosses between regions. Physical cards are on sale again after a chip shortage in 2023 and 2024; visitors can also buy a Welcome Suica at airports and major stations, or add a Suica to an iPhone's Wallet and top it up with a credit card, which is what I did.
- **Some cash.** Cards are now accepted widely, but small restaurants, temple and shrine offerings, market stalls, and the ticket machines at many ramen shops still want yen in coins and notes. Convenience-store ATMs, 7-Eleven's in particular, generally take foreign cards.
- **The Shinkansen.** The Nozomi, the fastest service on the Tōkaidō line, runs between Tokyo, Kyoto, and Osaka several times an hour. Reserve a seat on the right side heading west, seat E, for a chance at Mt Fuji between Mishima and Shin-Fuji. Bags larger than 160 cm in total dimensions need a seat reserved with an oversized-baggage area, or there is a ¥1,000 charge. For a single run from Tokyo to Osaka, individual tickets are usually cheaper than the national Japan Rail Pass, whose price rose sharply in 2023; do the arithmetic for your own route.
