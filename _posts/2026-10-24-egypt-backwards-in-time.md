On a wall at Philae, near Aswan, there is a short inscription carved in AD 394. It is the last dated text anyone wrote in Egyptian hieroglyphs. No later one has been found, and the script that had recorded some 3,500 years of kings went silent. I stood in front of it on my second day in Egypt, and only later realized that the rest of my trip ran in the opposite direction: from the last word of that civilization back to its first stone building. Seen that way, a week in Egypt is less a tour of monuments than a walk upstream through time.

## Cairo: the treasure before the tombs

Day 1 · Monday 20 November
{: .dayline}

I began at the Egyptian Museum on Tahrir Square with an Egyptologist as a guide. Starting with the treasure feels backwards, but it is the right order: the tombs of Upper Egypt are mostly empty, and their contents were here. Tutankhamun's gold mask draws the crowds, but the more useful lesson was scale. Tutankhamun was a minor king who died at about 19, and his burial alone filled rooms. That made it easier to imagine what the great pharaohs, whose tombs were robbed in antiquity, once took with them.

The museum has changed since my visit. Most of the Tutankhamun collection, including the mask, has moved to the Grand Egyptian Museum near Giza, which opened fully in November 2025 and shows the whole burial in one place for the first time.

## Aswan: the frontier

Day 2 · Tuesday 21 November
{: .dayline}

Aswan was ancient Egypt's southern border, the point where Egypt ended and Nubia began. The modern story starts at the High Dam, built under Nasser in the 1960s. From the top you see the river held back in Lake Nasser, one of the largest man-made lakes in the world. The dam ended the annual flood that had organized Egyptian life since prehistory; the ancient calendar itself had three seasons, named for the inundation, the growing, and the harvest. The lake behind the dam drowned much of Nubia, displacing its villages and forcing an international rescue of the temples in its path.

![Columns of the temple of Isis at Philae](/assets/images/egypt-2023/philae.jpg)
*Philae, the temple of Isis, rebuilt on Agilkia Island.*
{: .caption}

Philae was one of them. In the UNESCO campaign, the temple of Isis was dismantled and rebuilt block by block on higher ground on Agilkia Island, which is why the most romantic site in Egypt is also, in a sense, a reconstruction. Its importance lies not in its age, since most of what stands is Ptolemaic and Roman, but in its longevity. Isis was still worshipped here long after Christianity became Rome's official religion, and the temple stayed open until Justinian closed it around AD 537.

![Brightly painted Nubian houses](/assets/images/egypt-2023/nubian-village.jpg)
*A Nubian village on the west bank near Aswan.*
{: .caption}

The afternoon visit to a Nubian village gave the other side of the dam's story. Nubian houses are painted in bright colors and patterns, and Nubian languages are still spoken at home. Over tea, it was hard not to think about the inversion of history here. Around 750 BC, Nubian kings from the south conquered Egypt and ruled it as the 25th Dynasty, pharaohs that Egypt's own records tended to underplay.

I stayed at the Old Cataract, a Victorian hotel that Thomas Cook built in 1899 for its Nile travelers. Agatha Christie is said to have written part of *Death on the Nile* on its terrace. The terrace at sunset is still the hotel's real attraction, and I would not swap it for anything newer.

## Abu Simbel: propaganda in sandstone

Day 3 · Wednesday 22 November
{: .dayline}

The next morning I flew south to Abu Simbel, about 40 kilometers from the Sudanese border. Ramesses II cut the temple into the cliff to celebrate his victory over the Hittites at Kadesh around 1274 BC. Its four seated colossi, each about 20 meters high, wear the double crown of Upper and Lower Egypt. Set at the southern frontier, they told Nubia who was in charge.

![The seated colossi of Ramesses II at Abu Simbel](/assets/images/egypt-2023/abu-simbel.jpg)
*The colossi of Ramesses II. One lost its upper body in an ancient earthquake; the head still lies at its feet.*
{: .caption}

The historian's footnote is that Kadesh was not a victory. It was close to a draw, and some 15 years later Egypt and the Hittites signed what is often cited as the earliest surviving peace treaty. Abu Simbel is therefore one of history's great works of political messaging. It is also a survivor. In the 1960s, UNESCO cut the whole temple into blocks and moved it 65 meters higher to save it from the same lake I had looked at the day before. The temple faces east, and twice a year the rising sun still reaches the statues in its inner sanctuary.

![The Old Cataract hotel seen from a felucca on the Nile](/assets/images/egypt-2023/old-cataract-felucca.jpg)
*The Old Cataract from the river.*
{: .caption}

Back in Aswan, the Nubian Museum filled in the history the dam had submerged. Then, for no purpose at all, a felucca at sunset: the traditional Nile sailboat, with no engine, only wind, sand dunes on the west bank and the Old Cataract lit up on the bluff. It was the best hour of the trip.

## The road north: three temples of the last pharaohs

Day 4 · Thursday 23 November
{: .dayline}

The 3.5-hour drive to Luxor follows the river past three temples built by the Ptolemies, the Macedonian Greek dynasty that ruled Egypt after Alexander and adopted the full costume of pharaonic kingship. Kom Ombo is unusual because it is a double temple, split between the crocodile god Sobek and the falcon god Haroeris. Next door, a small museum holds mummified crocodiles, which the priests once kept and buried.

![Granite statue of Horus as a falcon at Edfu](/assets/images/egypt-2023/edfu-horus.jpg)
*Horus as a falcon, at the temple of Edfu.*
{: .caption}

Edfu, dedicated to Horus, is the best-preserved temple in Egypt. Granite falcons of the god stand at its doors, and its pylon shows Ptolemy XII smiting his enemies before Horus, an image any pharaoh 1,500 years earlier would have recognized. Esna, dedicated to the creator god Khnum, sits below the level of the modern town. Its hall, decorated largely under the Romans, has been cleaned in recent years to reveal color that was invisible for centuries.

## Luxor, West Bank: the houses of eternity

Day 5 · Friday 24 November
{: .dayline}

Thebes, modern Luxor, was the religious capital of the New Kingdom, the period from about 1550 to 1070 BC when Egypt was at its height. Egyptians built for the living on the east bank and buried their dead on the west, where the sun sets. The New Kingdom's kings stopped building pyramids, which had proven too easy to rob, and cut their tombs instead into a hidden valley on the west bank.

In the Valley of the Kings, the tomb of Seti I, father of Ramesses II, has the finest painted reliefs I saw in Egypt. Nearby, Hatshepsut's terraced temple at Deir el-Bahari reads almost as modern architecture. She ruled as pharaoh for about 20 years, and her successor later tried to erase her from the record.

![Hatshepsut's terraced temple below the cliffs of Deir el-Bahari](/assets/images/egypt-2023/hatshepsut.jpg)
*Hatshepsut's temple at Deir el-Bahari.*
{: .caption}

The tomb of Nefertari, Ramesses II's chief queen, has colors that look as if they were painted last year.

![Painted wall of Nefertari's tomb](/assets/images/egypt-2023/nefertari.jpg)
*The tomb of Nefertari in the Valley of the Queens. The queen, right, is led by the hand by a falcon-headed god.*
{: .caption}

I spent the afternoon at the Winter Palace, which opened in 1907. Lord Carnarvon, who financed Howard Carter's excavation, was a regular guest. After the tomb of Tutankhamun was found in 1922, the hotel became the base for the world's press, and Carter posted news of the find on its noticeboard. The story had come full circle from the museum on the first day.

## Luxor, East Bank: the temples of the living

Day 6 · Saturday 25 November
{: .dayline}

Karnak is less a temple than a city of temples, enlarged by king after king for some 2,000 years. The 134 columns of the Great Hypostyle Hall are the reason to go, and the scale is difficult to convey. Standing among them, you feel the purpose of the design, which was to make a human feel small before Amun. Hatshepsut's obelisk still stands here too, a reminder that her successor's erasure did not entirely succeed.

![Colossus of Ramesses II in the courtyard of Luxor Temple](/assets/images/egypt-2023/luxor-temple.jpg)
*Ramesses II again, in the courtyard he added to Luxor Temple.*
{: .caption}

Luxor Temple, begun under Amenhotep III around 1400 BC, carries the marks of the religious crisis that followed, when his son Akhenaten abandoned Amun for the single sun disc of the Aten. The Luxor Museum, small and well lit, was the best-curated collection of the trip. That evening I flew back to Cairo.

## Saqqara, Dahshur, Giza: where it began

Day 7 · Sunday 26 November
{: .dayline}

I saved the oldest monuments for the last day. The Step Pyramid of Djoser at Saqqara, designed by the architect Imhotep around 2670 BC, is the oldest large stone building in the world. It is six mastabas stacked into a staircase to the sky, and you can see the idea being invented.

![The Step Pyramid of Djoser at Saqqara](/assets/images/egypt-2023/step-pyramid.jpg)
*The Step Pyramid of Djoser at Saqqara.*
{: .caption}

At Dahshur, the Bent Pyramid of Sneferu shows that invention going wrong. Partway up, the builders reduced the angle, probably because the structure was becoming unstable. Sneferu then built the Red Pyramid nearby with the correction applied, and his son Khufu took the lesson to Giza.

![The Bent Pyramid of Sneferu at Dahshur](/assets/images/egypt-2023/bent-pyramid.jpg)
*The Bent Pyramid at Dahshur. The change of angle is visible about halfway up.*
{: .caption}

![The Sphinx with the pyramids of Khafre and Khufu behind it](/assets/images/egypt-2023/giza-sphinx.jpg)
*The Sphinx, probably carved for Khafre, with his pyramid and Khufu's behind it.*
{: .caption}

The Great Pyramid remained the tallest man-made structure on earth for nearly 4,000 years. Inside, the climb up the Grand Gallery to the King's Chamber is steep, hot, and cramped, and the chamber itself is bare granite. That emptiness was the right ending. Everything I had seen over the week, from Abu Simbel's colossi to the last hieroglyphs at Philae, descends from the confidence this building expressed.
