On a wall at Philae, near Aswan, there is a short inscription carved in AD 394. It is the last dated text anyone wrote in Egyptian hieroglyphs. No later one has been found, and the script that had recorded some 3,500 years of kings went silent. I stood in front of it in November 2023, and only later realized that the week had covered nearly the whole arc of that civilization, from its first stone building to its last word. So I have written it up in the order of Egypt's history rather than the order of my route.

## The Periods, and Where I Saw Them

Egyptologists divide ancient Egypt into kingdoms, when one king ruled the whole Nile valley, and intermediate periods, when that unity broke down. The dates below are approximate.

|---|---|---|

| First Intermediate and Middle Kingdom | c. 2181–1650 BC | — |

## The Nile and the Two Lands

Everything in Egypt depends on the Nile. The river flows north, from Aswan to the Mediterranean, so Upper Egypt is the south, upstream, and Lower Egypt is the north, the delta. Egyptians called their country Kemet, "the black land" <span class="hg" role="img" aria-label="Kemet in hieroglyphs">𓆎𓅓𓏏𓊖</span>, after the dark silt the flood left on the fields. The desert on either side was Deshret, "the red land."

Each half had its own emblems, and they appear on almost everything I saw:

<ul class="glyphs">
<li><span class="hg" role="img" aria-label="White crown in hieroglyphs">𓋑</span>White crown: Upper Egypt</li>
<li><span class="hg" role="img" aria-label="Red crown in hieroglyphs">𓋔</span>Red crown: Lower Egypt</li>
<li><span class="hg" role="img" aria-label="Double crown in hieroglyphs">𓋖</span>Double crown: the king of both</li>
<li><span class="hg" role="img" aria-label="Lotus in hieroglyphs">𓆸</span>Lotus: the plant of Upper Egypt</li>
<li><span class="hg" role="img" aria-label="Papyrus in hieroglyphs">𓇉</span>Papyrus: the plant of Lower Egypt</li>
<li><span class="hg" role="img" aria-label="King of Upper and Lower Egypt in hieroglyphs">𓇓𓆤</span>"King of Upper and Lower Egypt": the sedge of the south and the bee of the north</li>
</ul>

The two plants carry the same idea into architecture. Temple columns are carved as bundles of papyrus or lotus, and the sides of royal thrones, including those of the colossi at Abu Simbel, show the sema-tawy, "uniting the Two Lands": a lotus and a papyrus tied around the sign for "unite" <span class="hg" role="img" aria-label="Sema in hieroglyphs">𓄥</span>.

Papyrus was also Egypt's paper. Scribes cut the plant's pith into strips, laid them crosswise, and pressed them into sheets, and the Greek word for the plant, *papyros*, gave English the word *paper*. Carved hieroglyphs were the formal script for monuments; on papyrus, scribes wrote faster, cursive forms.

Royal names were written inside a cartouche, an oval loop of rope that protected the name it enclosed. I have drawn a few in this post, with the signs in a single line for simplicity.

## Old Kingdom: The Pyramid Age

c. 2686–2181 BC · Memphis, Saqqara, Dahshur, Giza · Day 1
{: .dayline}

Egypt became one country around 3100 BC, when the kings of Upper Egypt took Lower Egypt. They ruled from Memphis, near modern Cairo. By the Old Kingdom the king was treated as a god on earth, and the state's main project was his tomb. I started where that project started.

Little of Memphis survives above ground, largely because later builders carried off its stone. At its open-air museum, a limestone colossus of Ramesses II lies on its back, a reminder that the city stayed a royal seat long after the pyramid age.

![Fallen limestone colossus of Ramesses II at Memphis](/assets/images/egypt-2023/memphis-ramesses.jpg)
*The fallen colossus of Ramesses II at Memphis.*
{: .caption}

The Step Pyramid of Djoser at Saqqara, designed by the architect Imhotep around 2670 BC, is the oldest large stone building in the world. It is six mastabas stacked into a staircase to the sky, and you can see the idea being invented.

![The Step Pyramid of Djoser at Saqqara](/assets/images/egypt-2023/step-pyramid.jpg)
*The Step Pyramid of Djoser at Saqqara.*
{: .caption}

At Dahshur, the Bent Pyramid of Sneferu <span class="cartouche" role="img" aria-label="Sneferu in hieroglyphs">𓋴𓄤𓂋𓅱</span> shows that invention going wrong. Partway up, the builders reduced the angle, probably because the structure was becoming unstable. Sneferu then built the Red Pyramid nearby with the correction applied, and his son Khufu <span class="cartouche" role="img" aria-label="Khufu in hieroglyphs">𓐍𓅱𓆑𓅱</span> took the lesson to Giza.

![The Bent Pyramid of Sneferu at Dahshur](/assets/images/egypt-2023/bent-pyramid.jpg)
*The Bent Pyramid at Dahshur. The change of angle is visible about halfway up.*
{: .caption}

The Great Pyramid remained the tallest man-made structure on earth for nearly 4,000 years. Inside, the climb up the Grand Gallery to the King's Chamber is steep, hot, and cramped, and the chamber itself is bare granite. That emptiness is the point. Everything else I saw that week, from Abu Simbel's colossi to the last hieroglyphs at Philae, descends from the confidence this building expressed.

![The Sphinx with the pyramids of Khafre and Khufu behind it](/assets/images/egypt-2023/giza-sphinx.jpg)
*The Sphinx, probably carved for Khafre <span class="cartouche" role="img" aria-label="Khafre in hieroglyphs">𓇳𓈍𓆑</span>, with his pyramid and Khufu's behind it.*
{: .caption}

The Sphinx has gone by several names. By the New Kingdom, Egyptians worshipped it as Horemakhet, "Horus in the horizon," and Thutmose IV cleared the sand from its body and set up the Dream Stela between its paws. In Arabic it is Abu al-Hol, "the father of dread." "Sphinx" itself is a Greek word.

Lunch was at Khufu's, a restaurant on the Giza plateau whose terrace looks straight at the Great Pyramid. Its chef, Mostafa Seif, reworks the rural dishes he grew up with. The restaurant has since been named the best in Egypt and ranked fourth on the Middle East and North Africa's 50 Best list for 2025. I ordered hamam, roast pigeon. Pigeon is a staple of Egyptian cooking, and the mud-brick dovecotes that house the birds are still a common sight in villages along the Nile.

## New Kingdom: Empire, and the Houses of Eternity

c. 1550–1069 BC · Luxor, Abu Simbel, Cairo · Days 1, 3, 5, and 6
{: .dayline}

The Old Kingdom ended in more than a century of division. Kings from Thebes, modern Luxor, reunited the country around 2055 BC in what is now called the Middle Kingdom. That unity broke again when the Hyksos, rulers of Near Eastern origin, took the north, and another Theban dynasty expelled them around 1550 BC. The New Kingdom that followed was Egypt at its height: an empire reaching into Nubia and the Levant, run from Thebes and devoted to its god, Amun.

### Karnak: A City of Temples

Karnak is less a temple than a city of temples, enlarged by king after king for some 2,000 years. The 134 columns of the Great Hypostyle Hall, completed under Seti I and Ramesses II, are the reason to go, and the scale is difficult to convey. Standing among them, you feel the purpose of the design, which was to make a human feel small before Amun <span class="hg" role="img" aria-label="Amun in hieroglyphs">𓇋𓏠𓈖</span>, whose name means "the hidden one." The columns of the central aisle are carved as open papyrus flowers.

The kings of this period also changed how they were buried. Egyptians built for the living on the east bank and buried their dead on the west, where the sun sets. Pyramids had proven too easy to rob, so the New Kingdom's kings cut their tombs instead into a hidden valley on the west bank, the Valley of the Kings.

### Hatshepsut

Hatshepsut ruled as pharaoh for about 20 years, from around 1479 BC. Her terraced temple at Deir el-Bahari reads almost as modern architecture. Her successor, Thutmose III, later tried to erase her from the record, but her obelisk still stands at Karnak, a reminder that the erasure did not entirely succeed.

![Hatshepsut's terraced temple below the cliffs of Deir el-Bahari](/assets/images/egypt-2023/hatshepsut.jpg)
*Hatshepsut's temple at Deir el-Bahari.*
{: .caption}

### Amenhotep III, Akhenaten, and Tutankhamun

Luxor Temple, begun under Amenhotep III around 1390 BC, carries the marks of the religious crisis that followed. His son Akhenaten abandoned Amun for the single sun disc of the Aten and moved the capital to a new city. The Luxor Museum, small and well lit, was the best-curated collection of the trip.

The crisis ended with Tutankhamun <span class="cartouche" role="img" aria-label="Tutankhamun in hieroglyphs">𓇋𓏠𓈖𓏏𓅱𓏏𓋹</span>, who was born Tutankhaten, changed his name, and restored Amun. The name means "living image of Amun." Amun's name is written first in the cartouche out of respect, and the ankh <span class="hg" role="img" aria-label="Ankh in hieroglyphs">𓋹</span>, the sign for life, sits at the end.

Tutankhamun was a minor king who died at about 19, yet his burial alone filled rooms of the Egyptian Museum on Tahrir Square, where I saw it with an Egyptologist on my first day. That made it easier to imagine what the great pharaohs, whose tombs were robbed in antiquity, once took with them. *Update, 2026:* since my visit, most of the collection, including the gold mask, has moved to the Grand Egyptian Museum near Giza, which opened fully in November 2025 and shows the whole burial in one place for the first time.

I also went down into his tomb in the Valley of the Kings. It is small for a king's, and only the burial chamber is painted, but the treasure I had seen in Cairo came out of these few rooms, and the king's mummy still lies here.

![Painted burial chamber of Tutankhamun's tomb with his stone sarcophagus](/assets/images/egypt-2023/tutankhamun-tomb.jpg)
*Tutankhamun's burial chamber. On the right, his successor Ay performs the Opening of the Mouth ritual on the king, shown as Osiris; the stone sarcophagus is in the foreground.*
{: .caption}

That afternoon I was back at the Winter Palace, which opened in 1907. Lord Carnarvon, who financed Howard Carter's excavation, was a regular guest. After the tomb was found in 1922, the hotel became the base for the world's press, and Carter posted news of the find on its noticeboard.

![Sunset over the Nile from the Winter Palace in Luxor](/assets/images/egypt-2023/winter-palace-sunset.jpg)
*Sunset over the Nile from the Winter Palace.*
{: .caption}

### Seti I and Ramesses II

The tomb of Seti I has the finest painted reliefs I saw in Egypt. His son Ramesses II <span class="cartouche" role="img" aria-label="Ramesses in hieroglyphs">𓇳𓄟𓋴𓋴</span>, whose name means "Ra bore him," reigned for 66 years, and he is everywhere: in the courtyard he added to Luxor Temple, in the tomb of his chief queen, Nefertari, and above all at Abu Simbel.

![Colossus of Ramesses II in the courtyard of Luxor Temple](/assets/images/egypt-2023/luxor-temple.jpg)
*Ramesses II in the courtyard he added to Luxor Temple.*
{: .caption}

Nefertari's tomb in the Valley of the Queens has colors that look as if they were painted last year.

![Painted wall of Nefertari's tomb](/assets/images/egypt-2023/nefertari.jpg)
*The tomb of Nefertari. The queen, right, is led by the hand by a falcon-headed god.*
{: .caption}

Abu Simbel sits about 40 kilometers from the Sudanese border, and I flew there from Aswan. Ramesses II cut the temple into the cliff to celebrate his victory over the Hittites at Kadesh around 1274 BC. Its four seated colossi, each about 20 meters high, wear the double crown of Upper and Lower Egypt. Set at the southern frontier, they told Nubia who was in charge.

![The seated colossi of Ramesses II at Abu Simbel](/assets/images/egypt-2023/abu-simbel.jpg)
*The colossi of Ramesses II. One lost its upper body in an ancient earthquake; the head still lies at its feet.*
{: .caption}

The historian's footnote is that Kadesh was not a victory. It was close to a draw, and some 15 years later Egypt and the Hittites signed what is often cited as the earliest surviving peace treaty. Abu Simbel is therefore one of history's great works of political messaging. It is also a survivor. In the 1960s, UNESCO cut the whole temple into blocks and moved it 65 meters higher to save it from the lake behind the new Aswan High Dam. The temple faces east, and twice a year the rising sun still reaches the statues in its inner sanctuary.

## Third Intermediate and Late Period: Nubia's Turn

c. 1069–332 BC · Aswan · Days 2 and 3
{: .dayline}

After the New Kingdom, Egypt split again, and this time the south took over. Aswan was ancient Egypt's southern border, the point where Egypt ended and Nubia began. Around 750 BC, Nubian kings from beyond that border conquered Egypt and ruled it as the 25th Dynasty, pharaohs that Egypt's own records tended to underplay. Assyrian invasions pushed them back south within a century, and Egypt then passed through Persian rule before Alexander the Great arrived in 332 BC.

The Nubian Museum in Aswan tells that story, and it also tells the modern one. The High Dam, built under Nasser in the 1960s, holds the river back in Lake Nasser, one of the largest man-made lakes in the world. It ended the annual flood that had organized Egyptian life since prehistory; the ancient calendar itself had three seasons, named for the inundation, the growing, and the harvest. The lake drowned much of Nubia, displacing its villages and forcing an international rescue of the temples in its path, Abu Simbel and Philae among them.

![Brightly painted Nubian houses](/assets/images/egypt-2023/nubian-village.jpg)
*A Nubian village on the west bank near Aswan.*
{: .caption}

An afternoon in a Nubian village gave the other side of the dam's story. Nubian houses are painted in bright colors and patterns, and Nubian languages are still spoken at home. Over tea, I kept thinking about the inversion of history the 25th Dynasty represents: for once, Nubia ruled Egypt.

I stayed at the Old Cataract, a Victorian hotel that Thomas Cook built in 1899 for its Nile travelers. Agatha Christie is said to have written part of *Death on the Nile* on its terrace. The terrace at sunset is still the hotel's real attraction, and I would not swap it for anything newer. The best view of it, though, is from a felucca, the traditional Nile sailboat, which has no engine and moves only with the wind. At sunset, with sand dunes on the west bank and the hotel lit up on the bluff, it was the best hour of the trip.

![The Old Cataract hotel seen from a felucca on the Nile](/assets/images/egypt-2023/old-cataract-felucca.jpg)
*The Old Cataract from the river.*
{: .caption}

## Ptolemaic Egypt: Greek Kings in Pharaohs' Dress

332–30 BC · Kom Ombo, Edfu · Day 4
{: .dayline}

After Alexander died, his general Ptolemy took Egypt, and his descendants ruled it for nearly three centuries, until Cleopatra VII died in 30 BC. They were Macedonian Greeks, but they adopted the full costume of pharaonic kingship and built some of the largest temples in Egypt. Three of them line the 3.5-hour drive from Aswan to Luxor.

Kom Ombo is unusual because it is a double temple, split between the crocodile god Sobek and the falcon god Haroeris. Next door, a small museum holds mummified crocodiles, which the priests once kept and buried.

Edfu, dedicated to Horus, is the best-preserved temple in Egypt. Granite falcons of the god stand at its doors, and its pylon shows Ptolemy XII smiting his enemies before Horus, an image any pharaoh 1,500 years earlier would have recognized.

![Granite statue of Horus as a falcon at Edfu](/assets/images/egypt-2023/edfu-horus.jpg)
*Horus as a falcon, at the temple of Edfu.*
{: .caption}

![Johnew with two local men among the carved columns of Edfu's hypostyle hall](/assets/images/egypt-2023/edfu-hypostyle.jpg)
*My best impression of an archaeologist, among the columns at Edfu.*
{: .caption}

## Roman Egypt: Where the Writing Ended

30 BC–AD 395 · Esna, Philae · Days 2 and 4
{: .dayline}

Rome took Egypt from Cleopatra, and Roman emperors in turn had themselves carved as pharaohs. Esna, the third temple on the drive north, is dedicated to the creator god Khnum and sits below the level of the modern town. Its hall, decorated largely under the Romans, has been cleaned in recent years to reveal color that was invisible for centuries.

Philae is where the story ends. In the UNESCO campaign, the temple of Isis was dismantled and rebuilt block by block on higher ground on Agilkia Island, which is why the most romantic site in Egypt is also, in a sense, a reconstruction. Its importance lies not in its age, since most of what stands is Ptolemaic and Roman, but in its longevity. Isis was still worshipped here long after Christianity became Rome's official religion, and the temple stayed open until Justinian closed it around AD 537.

![The first pylon of the temple of Isis at Philae](/assets/images/egypt-2023/philae-pylon.jpg)
*The first pylon at Philae, where Ptolemy XII smites his enemies before Isis, as he does at Edfu.*
{: .caption}

![Columns of the temple of Isis at Philae](/assets/images/egypt-2023/philae.jpg)
*Philae, the temple of Isis, rebuilt on Agilkia Island.*
{: .caption}

The inscription of AD 394 is a few lines on a wall here. After it, Egyptians wrote their language in Coptic, an alphabet based on Greek, and no one could read hieroglyphs for some 1,400 years.

![The Rosetta Stone in the British Museum](/assets/images/egypt-2023/rosetta-stone.jpg)
*The Rosetta Stone, British Museum.*
{: .caption}

The key turned out to be a Ptolemaic decree. The Rosetta Stone, which French soldiers found in 1799 and which has been in the British Museum since 1802, records a decree of 196 BC in three scripts: hieroglyphs, Demotic, and Greek. Because the Greek could be read, Jean-François Champollion could match the royal names in its cartouches, Ptolemy <span class="cartouche" role="img" aria-label="Ptolemy in hieroglyphs">𓊪𓏏𓅱𓃭𓅓𓇋𓇋𓋴</span> first, and then Cleopatra on an obelisk taken from Philae itself. He announced the decipherment in 1822. Some 3,000 years after Djoser's pyramid, Philae is where the writing ended, and a name carved under the Ptolemies is where it was read again.
