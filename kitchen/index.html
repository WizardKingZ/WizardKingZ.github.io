Kitchen notes · what my recipes keep teaching me

Go by thermometer, not the clock

These are the rules behind the braises, broths, steaks and wok dishes I cook most. Each one points back to the recipes where it matters.

1 · SaltFirst, and by weight. Dry-brine the skin a day ahead, as in the Shandong chicken and the fig chicken.
2 · AcidThe finish that makes a rich dish taste light: black vinegar in the Shandong sauce, lime in Marion's steak salad.
3 · UmamiPair a glutamate with a nucleotide. Dashi is kombu plus bonito, and the beef noodle soup stacks tomato, soy and bean paste on beef.
4 · FatCarries aroma. The duck fat and lard oil in the shoyu ramen and the rendered fat trimming in the wagyu do the heavy lifting.
5 · Heat & browningDry surfaces, hot pan, no crowding. The reverse sear and the pad kra pao both live or die on this.

Braising · my biggest habit

Barely trembling, never boiling

Collagen starts melting around 70 °C / 158 °F and speeds up toward 82 °C / 180 °F. Hold the liquid at 85–90 °C / 185–194 °F on the lowest burner or in a 135–150 °C / 275–300 °F oven for 2–3 hours.

Where: beef noodle soup, oxtail, galbi-jjim, lamb shanks, pork knuckle. All are better the next day.
Dashi · the base I reuse

Kombu low, bonito off the heat

Bring 30 g kombu up slowly and lift it out at about 80 °C / 176 °F, then steep 25 g bonito off the heat for 5 minutes. Don't press the flakes.

Builds: miso soup, seafood chashu udon, and any Japanese braise that needs depth.
Salt by weight

Not all teaspoons are equal

Diamond Crystal kosher salt is about 3 g per tsp, Morton kosher about 5 g, table salt about 6 g. Pasta water for the scoglio wants about 7 g per litre.

House salt: "kosher salt" in these recipes means Diamond Crystal. Use about half the volume of Morton or table salt.
Frying · twice is the trick

Low, rest, then hot

Fry once at 160 °C / 320 °F to cook through, rest, then again at 180 °C / 355 °F to crisp. The second fry drives off the moisture the first one left.

Where: karaage, tebasaki; tonkatsu goes once at 170 °C / 340 °F.
Spices & chilli

Toast, then bloom in fat

Toast whole spices dry for 1–3 minutes and bloom ground spices in oil for 20–40 s. Capsaicin lives in the membranes and dissolves in fat and alcohol.

Where: the cumin crust on the lamb skewers, the spice bag in the beef noodle soup, the curry paste in the green curry.
Ramen · my two-day project

Tare names the bowl

Each bowl of my double soup shoyu gets 30–45 ml tare, 1 tbsp aroma oil and 400 ml boiling soup, then noodles drained hard.

Rule: a weak tare can't be rescued by a strong broth. Taste the tare on its own first.

The bar · what I pour

Three templates run my bar

My drinks come from three of the six classic templates. The Old Fashioned is the stirred one, the spicy margarita is a Sidecar, and the mojito is a Daiquiri made long. The rest of the six are here for reference.

House · stirred

Old Fashioned, eight ways

60 ml spirit, 7.5 ml rich demerara syrup, 2 dashes Angostura and 1 orange, stirred 30 seconds over one big cube. Swap in rye, Japanese whisky, Islay, aged rum, mezcal or Cognac.

Zero-proof: cold-brewed Lapsang and Assam tea with alcohol-free bitters.
House · shaken

Spicy margarita

I infuse a whole bottle of blanco tequila with jalapeño instead of muddling chilli into each drink. Seeded slices are ready in 15–20 minutes, so taste as it goes, then strain.

Rim: salt on the outside of the glass only, so it doesn't fall into the drink.
House · long

Mojito

45 ml white rum, 20 ml lime, 10 mint leaves and 2 tsp sugar, topped with soda. Press the mint gently, because torn leaves turn bitter.

With: it's what I pour with the pad thai and Thai steak salad.

Hosting

Always one zero-proof

The tea Old Fashioned, lime soda with a pinch of salt for the Thai dishes, or barley tea with the teriyaki salmon.

Ice

More ice, less water

Lots of dry, fresh ice chills faster with less dilution than a few half-melted cubes. One big cube for anything stirred and served on the rocks.

Keeping

Squeeze citrus the same day

Vermouth is wine: refrigerated, it keeps about two months. A US jigger holds about 44 ml, so I measure in ml.

How it works

Every recipe follows one format

I cook it, snap it or find it, and the home-chef skill writes it up in this format. Each recipe lands here, with a markdown copy and photo in my Project Cook Recipes folder.

PhotoMost of this collection started as a phone photo of dinner, like the galbi-jjim, the lobster and the pork knuckle bento. Claude reads the plate and writes the recipe.
ConvertPaste a recipe, a link or a cookbook page. Adam Liaw's tonkatsu and beef curry and Marion's steak salad came in this way.
Rough nameDescribe a dish from memory and get a best guess, like the tebasaki wings or the fig chicken.
My ownThe ones I already had written down, like my beef noodle soup, dashi and family oxtail soup, go in as written.
PostIt lands here, in the Recipes folder and, on request, in the Recipe Master workbook. The pantry updates itself.

Why it works · a quick reference

The reasoning behind the techniques, acids and herb pairings that keep showing up in my recipes. Each recipe also has its own "Why it works" box.

Techniques

TechniqueWhy it worksIn my recipes
Dry-briningSalt draws moisture out and is then reabsorbed. That seasons the meat inside and leaves a dry surface that crisps.Shandong chicken, fig chicken
SearingBrowning (the Maillard reaction) builds hundreds of flavour compounds, but only on a dry surface in a hot pan. A wet surface just steams.Dry-aged steak, braised chicken
Reverse searA low oven brings thick steak evenly to temperature and dries the surface. A quick, hard sear then browns it without overcooking the edge.Dry-aged steak
DeglazingLiquid poured into a hot pan lifts the fond, the browned bits stuck to the bottom, and turns them into the sauce. That's most of a sauce's depth.Oxtail, lamb shanks, duck breast, steak don
BraisingCollagen in tough cuts melts into gelatin at about 70–90 °C / 158–194 °F. Slow heat makes the meat tender and the sauce rich.Beef noodle soup, galbi-jjim, pork knuckle
Blanching meatA quick boil pulls out blood and scum, so long braises and broths stay clean and clear.Beef noodle soup, galbi-jjim, pork knuckle
Blooming in fatMost spice and curry-paste aromas dissolve in fat, not water. Fry them in oil first and the whole dish carries them.Green curry, beef curry, bean pastes
Double fryingThe first fry cooks the meat through. The second, hotter fry drives off the moisture that rose to the surface, so the crust stays crisp.Karaage, tebasaki
EmulsifyingStarch from pasta water, or cold butter swirled in off the heat, binds fat and liquid into a glossy sauce that clings.Scoglio, lamb shank sauce
RestingCarryover heat finishes the centre, and the meat loses less juice when you slice it.Steaks, roast chicken, duck

Acids and alcohol

IngredientWhy it worksIn my recipes
VinegarAcid cuts fat and makes rich food taste lighter. Black vinegar is malty, rice vinegar is clean, balsamic is sweet.Shandong sauce, Peking duck glaze, fig chicken
CitrusFresh acid plus aromatic oils in the zest. Add it at the end, because heat dulls both.Thai steak salad, lemon wings, scoglio
TamarindA fruity, rounder sourness than lime. It's the backbone of pad thai.Pad thai
Wine to deglazeAlcohol dissolves flavour compounds that water can't, and its acid brightens. Reduce it so the harsh alcohol cooks off.Oxtail, lamb shanks, scoglio
Shaoxing, sake, mirinCooking wines add aroma and tame strong meat and fish smells. Mirin adds sweetness and gloss.Three-cup chicken, teriyaki, ramen chashu
Spirits for infusingChilli heat (capsaicin) dissolves in alcohol, so a spirit picks it up fast and evenly.Spicy margarita
