In plain English

The idea. Stocks fall for two reasons: investors expect lower profits, or investors get more fearful and demand a higher return. Profit losses are permanent; fear tends to reverse, because a higher required return means higher returns later. A long-term investor should therefore demand much more pay for exposure to profit news (the "bad" beta) than to fear news (the "good" beta). The newer version adds a third risk: news that the market is about to get more turbulent.

How it becomes a risk premium. Each stock gets one beta for each kind of news, and each beta has a price. In the data, a unit of profit beta is paid % a month and a unit of fear beta %, a ratio of about seven to one. For the market as a whole, × % + × % gives an equity risk premium of about % a month (~% a year), against % actually earned.

What I find. The model gets the market right but not the 25 size and value portfolios: value stocks no longer carry more of the bad beta, and small growth stocks earn far less than their betas imply. Volatility news helps explain value, momentum and carry, but its price is not statistically reliable.

Introduction

It is well known that the CAPM fails to explain the average stock return in the modern period (after 1963) using the value-weighted market return as a proxy for the market portfolio. According to Sharpe (1964) and Lintner (1965), a single total wealth portfolio can summarize a stock's return characteristics with its beta. However, Fama and French (1992) documented the size and value anomalies, and indicated that portfolios tilting towards small and value stocks tend to generate excess returns. By solving Merton's (1973) intertemporal capital asset pricing model (ICAPM) within the Epstein and Zin (1989) utility framework of a representative agent, Campbell and Vuolteenaho (2004) provided an elegant explanation by splitting the beta risk into "bad" and "good" varieties: the beta associated with news about cash flows and the beta associated with news about discount rates. They showed some evidence that value stocks and small stocks have considerably higher cash-flow betas than growth stocks and large stocks. In other words, for a conservative long-term investor in the ICAPM framework, small and value stocks are intertemporal hedges and perform well when investment opportunities deteriorate; however, in equilibrium, these assets should have delivered a lower average return, so the rational long-term investor should not tilt their portfolio towards small and value stocks. Furthermore, in a subsequent paper, Campbell, Giglio, Polk and Turley (2018) claimed that either increasing volatility of stock returns or decreasing expected stock returns can send negative shocks to the investment opportunities in the stock market. In addition to the low-frequency movements in equity volatility, they proposed a three-beta ICAPM with cash-flow, discount-rate and risk news to explain the cross-sectional asset return puzzle, and provided results consistent with their "Bad Beta, Good Beta" paper.

In this paper, I examine whether the two-beta ICAPM in Campbell and Vuolteenaho (2004) and the three-beta ICAPM in Campbell et al. (2018) can still explain the size and value puzzle in the latest modern data (). Under both vector-autoregressive (VAR) economies, I estimate the parameters using the full sample data (). In the first two sections (tabs 01 and 02), I describe both ICAPM models and the data used to estimate the parameters of the VAR economies. In the third section (tabs 03 to 05), I walk through my estimated results on the 25 ME- and BE/ME-sorted portfolios from Ken French's website in the modern data, and compare them against the results in both of Campbell's papers. In the fourth section (tab 06), I conduct an out-of-sample test on some popular equity and currency trading strategies using the estimated two-beta and three-beta models. In the final section (tab 07), I summarize the findings.

In my VAR economy, I have six state variables: the excess market return (measured as the log excess return on the Center for Research in Security Prices (CRSP) value-weighted market over one-month Treasury bills, from Ken French's website); the expected market volatility, estimated using a lagged regression model; the log yield of the one-month Treasury bill; the market's smoothed price-earnings ratio (measured as the log ratio of the S&P 500 price index to a ten-year moving average of S&P 500 earnings from Shiller's website); the default credit spread (the log yield spread between Moody's BAA bonds and AAA bonds, obtained from the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis); and the small-stock value spread (measured as the difference between the log book-to-market ratios of small value and small growth stocks).

Tables II and IV report parameter estimates for the two-beta and three-beta VAR models respectively over the full sample period, June 1929 to . In the two-beta model, I exclude the expected market volatility to keep it consistent with the Campbell and Vuolteenaho (2004) formulation; in the three-beta model, all state variables are used. Ordinary least squares (OLS) standard errors are reported in brackets below the coefficients. Finally, I report the R² for each regression. Both tables show that the lagged market excess return, together with the other state variables, provides some ability to predict the future market return; the T-bill yield and the small-stock value spread have coefficients consistent with the results in Campbell and Vuolteenaho (2004). Campbell et al. (2018) show that expected volatility and the default credit spread positively predict the excess return. The expected volatility has a positive coefficient () but is not significant, and the default credit spread has a negative sign in the three-beta VAR economy, also insignificant. One can argue that market volatility tends to widen the credit spread, so EVOL explains the majority of the default spread, and the negative contribution from DEF provides some offsetting effect. The smoothed price-earnings ratio shows negative predictive power. Overall, an R² of about 2% for both forecasting models ( and ) is reasonable for a monthly return model.

Tables III and V summarize the behavior of the implied news about cash flows and discount rates from the two-beta model, and the implied news about cash flows, discount rates and volatility from the three-beta model, respectively. The estimated news about cash flows has a lower variance than the news about discount rates, which agrees with the finding of Campbell (1991) that discount-rate news is the dominant component of the market return.

Figures 1 and 2 illustrate the VAR model's view of stock market history in relation to NBER recessions. The shaded areas indicate NBER recession periods. My results are similar to Campbell's in the common period (1963–2001); during the 2008 financial crisis, I see both the news about discount rates and the news about cash flows drop. Campbell and Vuolteenaho (2004) call this a "mixed recession" (as in the Great Depression). In particular, the news about volatility peaked substantially in 2008, second only to the early 1930s.

In Panel B, the last matrix shows the estimated betas for the news about volatility. First of all, it is no surprise to see that the cash-flow betas are significantly lower than the discount-rate betas, because cash-flow news comes from VAR economies with limited forecastability. In Panel B, the volatility betas are positive for all 25 portfolios in the updated sample, so each portfolio partly hedges news of higher volatility; with a negative price of volatility risk, the portfolios with the lowest volatility betas are the riskiest. For both ICAPMs, the small stocks still show higher betas in both discount rates and cash flows. Moreover, the value stocks show lower discount-rate betas. However, the value stocks have lower cash-flow betas than the growth stocks. This suggests that the two innovations co-move in the modern period and that the two-beta model may have trouble disentangling the size-value puzzle. Based on the three-beta ICAPM, the volatility betas are lower for value stocks than for growth stocks, consistent with the story from the earlier sample in Campbell et al. (2018), but higher for small stocks than for large stocks. The lower hedging premium required for value stocks may explain the value anomaly in the modern period; it does not explain the size anomaly.

Method

  • EVOL. Next month's realized variance of daily returns, regressed with a constant on this month's RMe, PE, DEF, VS and realized variance; the fitted value is dated at the month whose data it uses.
  • VAR. OLS on the full sample; ρ = 0.951/12; the two-beta VAR omits EVOL.
  • Betas. One lag of news in the numerator; the variance of NCF − NDR in the denominator; bootstrap standard errors from 2,000 draws.
  • Cross-section. OLS without an intercept on average monthly excess returns; gDR fixed at the market variance.
  • Strategies. Excess returns as published; carry is already a long-short excess return.

Conclusions

Empirically, I find that value stocks have a considerably lower insurance premium to hedge future volatility shocks, and this can explain their higher average returns; the price of volatility risk, however, is not statistically significant (t-statistic of ), and small stocks do not show the same pattern. I cannot find clearly higher cash-flow betas for value stocks in either the two-beta or the three-beta ICAPM. Chen and Zhao (2009) argued that news about cash flows is computed residually relative to news about discount rates, so the cash-flow news captures all the noise in the VAR model. If a variable is omitted from but belongs to rational investors' information set, it ends up in the innovation of the cash-flow news. Therefore, the cash-flow news is very sensitive to the VAR specification. They showed that value companies do not have larger cash-flow betas under an alternative VAR specification. This is consistent with my beta estimates. Hence, the disagreement on cash-flow betas may stem from the misspecification of the VAR economies.

Limits and next steps

  • Negative variance forecasts. The linear EVOL regression gives a negative forecast in months; a floored or log-variance specification is the next test.
  • Cross-section design. A regression with an intercept and GLS weights, and rolling out-of-sample premia from 1990, would test the fit more fairly.
  • VAR specification. Because cash-flow news is a residual, an alternative state vector (for example with the term spread) could change the cash-flow betas.
