The short version

Overview

Asset-based finance (ABF) lends against pools of assets, such as consumer loans, equipment, aircraft, data centers and royalties, and is supported "first by the contractual cash flows of a pool of assets owned by a limited-purpose borrower, and then by the liquidation value of those assets themselves" (Apollo, interested party). The financial institutions group (FIG) sector comprises the firms that originate, fund, hold and manage financial assets: banks, insurers, asset and alternative managers, specialty finance companies and business development companies (BDCs). The two now form one funding chain. Originators make the loans, banks finance them in warehouses, alternative managers buy the pools and insurers hold the investment-grade notes, so ABF is best read as a product that runs through every FIG balance sheet.

Seven things to know

  1. ABF is large by any definition, but the capital dedicated to it is small. KKR sizes the market at over US$6tn, rising to US$9tn by 2029 (KKR filing); Apollo uses US$20tn-plus on a broad definition (Apollo); PIMCO counts about US$25tn of US loans and contractual cash-flow assets but only roughly US$200bn of third-party, non-captive ABF AUM (PIMCO). All three raise ABF capital and are interested parties. Closed-end ABF fundraising reached US$27.1bn in 2025, 16.4% of closed-end private credit against 10.6% in 2024 (McKinsey). See sections 3 and 5.
  2. Private structures let originators and banks move loans and risk off their balance sheets. Originators fund new loans in a bank warehouse, then refinance through term ABS, forward flow or whole-loan sales; the largest disclosed forward flow lets KKR buy up to €65bn of PayPal's European BNPL receivables through Mar 2028 (PayPal). Banks keep loans but sell the junior risk through SRT: they securitized €378bn of loans and protected €30bn of tranches in 2025 (IACPM). See sections 4, 7.2 and 7.4.
  3. Banks finance the nonbanks that compete with them. US commercial bank loans to nondepository financial institutions reached US$2,066.8bn in the week to Sep 23, 2026, up 11.9% y/y (Fed H.8). The FDIC's call-report measure, a different definition, was US$1.4tn at end-2025 (FDIC), and bank credit commitments to nonbanks totaled US$2.6tn in Q4 2025 (Fed FSR). See sections 7.1 and 9.3.
  4. Life insurers are the anchor buyers. US insurers held US$9.6tn of cash and invested assets at end-2025 (NAIC), about 13% of it in private credit (NAIC). Record US retail annuity sales of US$464.1bn in 2025 keep liabilities growing (LIMRA). See sections 5 and 10.
  5. Alternative managers turn permanent capital into the sector's widest margins. The largest listed alternative managers report fee-related earnings margins of 42–70% (see section 11), against about 30% for asset managers overall on global AuM of US$147tn (BCG). Perpetual capital makes up 60% of Apollo's AUM (Apollo 8-K). See sections 11 and 13.
  6. The largest recent ABF losses involved collateral that was allegedly missing or pledged twice. First Brands carried debt of over US$11bn, including US$6.2bn on and US$4.6bn off balance sheet, and converted to Chapter 7 on Aug 24, 2026 (BRAKE Report; BRAKE Report, both secondary). Tricolor cost JPMorgan US$170mm, Fifth Third US$178mm and Barclays US$150mm (Banking Dive, secondary). See section 16.
  7. Stress is now showing in semi-liquid funds and corporate private credit. Blue Owl's OTIC received redemption requests for 40.7% of shares in Q1 2026 against a 5% cap (PitchBook). Fitch's US private credit default rate reached 6.3% in the twelve months to Aug 2026 (Benzinga via Webull, citing Fitch, secondary). See sections 12 and 16.

Policy is pulling in two directions: the US Basel III endgame re-proposal of Mar 19, 2026 would ease bank capital (Freshfields, secondary), while new NAIC charges on residual tranches tighten insurer capital (Sidley, secondary). Section 15 sets out the rules and section 20 the calendar.

3

ABF definition and size

Part A · ABF

Asset-based finance has no agreed boundary, so its reported size runs from US$5.5tn to about US$25tn depending on what each source counts. The figures below come mostly from managers raising ABF capital, who are interested parties; each estimate is labeled with its definition.

3.1 What counts

ABF lends against pools of assets, not against a company's enterprise value. Apollo defines it as lending "supported first by the contractual cash flows of a pool of assets owned by a limited-purpose borrower, and then by the liquidation value of those assets themselves" (Apollo). KKR groups the collateral into hard assets, financial assets and contractual cash flows such as leases and royalties (KKR). The pools are typically self-amortizing, with front-loaded principal and a 1–5 year average life (KKR).

Four neighboring terms overlap with ABF and are often confused with it:

  • Asset-based lending (ABL) is a narrower corporate product: borrowing-base revolvers secured by a company's receivables and inventory. One forecast cited by Proskauer sizes it at US$609.6bn in 2022, rising to US$1.5tn by 2032 (Proskauer, secondary).
  • Asset-backed securities (ABS) are the tradeable, bond-form subsector of ABF (Guggenheim). Private ABF finances the same collateral through bilateral loans, warehouses and unrated or privately rated notes.
  • Specialty finance is a fundraising category that includes ABF. With Intelligence counted US$47.4bn of specialty finance closes in 2025, of which ABF was just under US$20bn (With Intelligence).
  • Direct lending finances operating companies against their cash flow, usually with interest-only bullet loans. In an Apr 2025 document, Brookfield puts direct lending at about US$3tn with about 50% nonbank penetration, against an ABF market of US$7tn with about 5% (Brookfield). Its later Sep 2025 presentation, citing Oliver Wyman, uses a narrower US$5.5tn US universe excluding real estate (Exhibit 4).

4

How ABF works

Part A · ABF

An ABF transaction moves a pool of loans from an originator into a bankruptcy-remote vehicle, funds it with layered debt while it grows, and then refinances it through a bond sale or a sale to a long-term buyer. Each step has its own lenders, documents and failure points.

4.1 Originators and the assets

Originators supply the assets and usually keep the customer relationship. They include banks, captive finance arms, fintech lenders such as Affirm, Upstart, Pagaya and Oportun, and specialty finance companies. Fintechs fund themselves through several channels at once: Affirm had US$28.2bn of committed funding capacity across warehouses, securitizations and forward flow at Mar 31, 2026 (Affirm). Large managers increasingly own originators outright. KKR had 18 captive ABF platforms in Jul 2025 (KKR), and Apollo's 16 proprietary platforms originated US$127bn of its US$309bn 2025 origination (Apollo). Owning the platform gives the manager "repeatable origination it controls, rather than being subject to competitive auctions" (Covenant Lite, secondary).

4.2 Warehouse funding

A warehouse is a revolving loan to a special-purpose vehicle, sized by a borrowing base of eligible assets. The borrower is "typically a special-purpose vehicle" isolated from its parent's credit (Mayer Brown, secondary). Lenders advance a percentage of eligible collateral, cap concentrations by borrower, geography, product or vintage, and exclude assets that fail eligibility tests (Axis Group Ventures, secondary).

Filed terms show the range. Oportun's four personal-loan warehouses total US$1.14bn, with a 95.0% advance rate (floor 92.0%), pricing of Term SOFR plus 2.56–3.18% and two- to three-year revolving periods (Oportun 10-K). ACV Auctions' US$125.0mm floorplan warehouse prices at Term SOFR plus 0.11448% plus 3.00%, with step-ups after an early amortization event (ACV 10-K). Market commentary puts prime consumer advance rates at 75–85% and pricing at SOFR plus 350–500bps for fintechs with 12–18 months' history (Axis Group Ventures, secondary). The FSB reports about 225bps for borrowing-base facilities and collateralized ABF leverage of up to 65% loan-to-value (FSB). No citable source publishes separate senior and mezzanine warehouse spreads.

Rule of thumb (author's): the advance rate sets the first-loss check. At an 85% senior advance, each US$100 of loans needs US$15 from a mezzanine lender or the originator (author's arithmetic).

Banks remain the main warehouse lenders. US commercial bank loans to nondepository financial institutions reached US$2,066.8bn in the week to Sep 23, 2026, up 11.9% y/y, on the Fed's weekly H.8 measure (Federal Reserve H.8). The FDIC's call-report measure, a different definition, was US$1.4tn at end-2025, or 5.6% of industry assets; its 35.2% rise in 2025 partly reflects reclassification (FDIC). Section 7.1 covers these series in depth.

4.3 Takeout: securitization, forward flow and whole-loan sales

The warehouse is temporary; three takeouts refinance it.

  • Term securitization sells the seasoned pool as rated bonds and repays the warehouse. Pagaya, for example, had issued over US$26bn of ABS by Feb 2025 (Pagaya).
  • Forward flow commits a buyer to purchase new loans as they are originated, usually with the seller keeping servicing. Purchase prices can include deferred consideration, sellers must repurchase ineligible assets and buyers often require minimum volumes (Weil, secondary). The largest disclosed agreement lets KKR buy up to €65bn of PayPal's European buy-now-pay-later receivables through Mar 2028 (PayPal). Pricing is rarely disclosed.
  • Whole-loan sales move an existing book in one trade. KKR agreed to acquire £5.2bn of NewDay card receivables in Sep 2025 (CVC), and HSBC sold an A$36bn Australian loan book to Blackstone in Jul 2026 (FinanceAsia, secondary).

4.4 Servicing, data and controls

The servicer collects the cash, so servicer integrity and data quality determine whether the collateral exists. Back-up servicers come in three grades: cold (monthly data, several weeks to take over), warm (about 30 days) and hot (parallel running with daily reconciliation), with fees such as US$3,000 a month on one 2025 deal (Academy Securities, secondary). The Tricolor and First Brands failures in 2025 involved collateral allegedly pledged to more than one lender (Willkie, secondary); section 16 covers both. The lender response has been granular collateral schedules, updated lien searches, controlled collection accounts, independent receivable verification and a rule against the company acting as sole servicer (Willkie, secondary).

4.5 Bank capital tools: SRT

In a significant risk transfer, a bank keeps its loans but sells the junior risk to investors in exchange for capital relief. Banks securitized €378bn of loans and protected €30bn of tranches in 2025 (IACPM). The Basel Committee counts about €750bn of protected assets, or 1.1% of bank assets, across Canada, the euro area, the UK and the US (BCBS). Relief is modest per deal, averaging 43bps of CET1 (BIS). Section 7.4 covers investors and regulation.

4.6 Fund-level finance

Fund finance lends to the funds themselves, adding a second layer of leverage above the asset-level structures. The Fund Finance Association estimates the market at about US$1.5tn (range US$1.4–1.75tn), with subscription lines alone at least US$1.1tn (FFA). Subscription lines are secured on investors' uncalled commitments; NAV facilities are secured on portfolio value, at loan-to-values "generally limited to 30%" (FSB). Rated note feeders and collateralized fund obligations turn fund interests into rated debt that insurers can hold; section 7.5 covers them.

5

Investors and demand drivers

Part A · ABF

Insurers are the anchor buyers of ABF, with pensions, sovereign funds, banks and retail vehicles filling specific layers. They buy for spread, amortization and diversification, and bank retrenchment and regulation keep supplying assets.

Insurers want long-dated investment-grade yield to match annuity liabilities. US insurers held US$9.6tn of cash and invested assets at end-2025 (NAIC), of which about 13% was private credit (NAIC). Privately placed ABS rose from 9.8% to 14.3% of life insurers' private placements between 2017 and 2024, and private-equity-owned insurers held over 40% of financial and ABS private placements in 2024 (Chicago Fed). Record US retail annuity sales of US$464.1bn in 2025 kept the liability side growing (LIMRA).

Closed-end funds are a smaller but faster-growing channel. Closed-end ABF fundraising reached US$27.1bn in 2025, 16.4% of closed-end private credit against 10.6% in 2024 (McKinsey). The largest fund, Ares Pathfinder III, closed at US$8.5bn in Jun 2026 (Ares).

Investors cite three reasons for buying, all argued mainly by managers:

  • Spread premium. PIMCO models a private-over-public premium of 240bps for ABF against 70bps for direct lending, and expected five-year returns of 9.4% against 8.5% (PIMCO, interested party).
  • Amortization. Principal returns monthly from thousands of obligors rather than at a single bullet maturity (KKR).
  • Diversification, with limits. PIMCO's own data show ABF correlation to high-yield factors of 0.59, rising to 0.83 in stress (PIMCO).

Three forces keep supplying assets. Bank retrenchment leaves room for nonbanks, especially in Europe, where their share of ABF is 13% against 34% in the US (see section 3.2). Regulation shapes the pace: banks have cut high-capital-charge loans in response to "stricter regulations, changes in loan accounting, and a rapid rise in interest rates" (PIMCO), but the US Basel III endgame re-proposal of Mar 19, 2026 would modestly lower system capital (Freshfields, secondary), which could slow the transfer. Rates cut both ways: the rapid rise PIMCO cites pushed banks away from some lending, while tight public spreads (see section 3.3) narrow the premium over public alternatives. 6

Asset classes

Part A · ABF

ABF collateral falls into four families, and their credit behavior differs far more than their legal structures do. KKR groups ABF into hard assets, financial assets and contractual cash flows such as leases and royalties (KKR, interested party). Its retail ABF interval fund shows a typical diversified mix: hard assets 24%, consumer and residential 23%, contractual cash flows 21%, commercial finance 14% and non-core holdings 11% at Apr 30, 2026 (KKR ABF Fund N-CSRS). By outstanding US balances, residential mortgages (US$13tn) and consumer credit (about US$5.2tn) dwarf equipment finance (US$1.4tn) and contractual cash-flow assets (about US$500bn) (PIMCO, interested party). Exhibit 10 summarizes the main asset classes, and Exhibit 11 tracks how they are performing.

6.1 Consumer

Consumer credit is the deepest ABF pool and the best measured. US household debt reached US$18.77tn in Q2 2026, with card balances up US$21bn (1.7%) in the quarter and card delinquency transitions "largely steady" (NY Fed).

  • Credit cards: bank-dominated, with improving losses. The bank card charge-off rate fell to 3.82% in Q2 2026 from 4.19% a year earlier (Federal Reserve via FRED). Card ABS trusts are stronger still: excess spread covered charge-offs about 10.0x in Jun 2026, against 2.0–3.0x before 2008, and monthly payment rates ran at about 38–42% (Capital Advisors Group, citing trust disclosures).
  • Auto: prime is stable and non-prime is deteriorating. US vehicle ABS issuance hit a record US$165bn in 2025, and captive finance arms issued about half of it (Inside Mortgage Finance). KBRA's July 2026 indices put prime annualized net losses at 0.66%, unchanged year on year, and non-prime at 9.46%, up 61bps (KBRA). The Tricolor collapse, now the subject of fraud charges (see section 16), hit this segment.
  • Unsecured personal loans: fintech-originated and fast-growing. Unsecured consumer loan ABS issuance rose 54% to US$25.6bn in 2025, and Fitch estimates fintechs originate about half of US personal loans (Asset Securitization Report, secondary). Warehouse lenders typically advance 75–85% against prime consumer installment loans, and some structures go to 90% or more at higher pricing (Axis Group Ventures, secondary).
  • BNPL: funded mainly by forward flow. PayPal, Affirm and Klarna sell receivables to private buyers while keeping the customer and the servicing (see section 7.2).
  • Private student loans: a new forward-flow market. KKR's ABF funds agreed in Nov 2025 to buy a seed portfolio plus at least US$2bn a year of new Sallie Mae loans for an initial three years, with Sallie Mae retaining servicing (Sallie Mae release).
  • Residential: second liens are scaling. Second-lien RMBS (HELOCs and closed-end seconds) reached US$30bn in 2025, and SFA expects about US$40bn in 2026; securitized delinquencies run at about 1.0% for closed-end seconds and about 1.5% for HELOCs (SFA). Home equity investments are smaller: the three largest providers originated about 54,000 agreements in 2015–2025, and at least ten states have acted on the product (HousingWire, secondary).
  • Residential solar: the clearest consumer stress. Sunnova and Solar Mosaic both filed for Chapter 11 in Jun 2025 (Solar Builder, secondary). KBRA's solar loan index showed annualized net losses of 3.93% in Mar 2026, up 132bps year on year (KBRA).

6.2 Commercial and SME

Commercial ABF is less transparent than consumer ABF, and its largest recent loss involved alleged receivables fraud. Equipment finance is the measured segment: ELFA reported Jul 2026 new business volume of US$14.3bn, up 47.3% year on year, with 30-day-plus delinquencies of 1.8% and charge-offs of 0.46% (ELFA). KKR launched the Akrapoint equipment finance platform with a US$350mm commitment in Sep 2026 (Alternative Credit Investor, secondary). Trade and supply chain receivables carry different risks, because a lender depends on invoices it cannot easily verify. First Brands showed how that fails (see section 16). No reliable volume or loss series was found for small business loans, merchant cash advances or fleet lending.

6.3 Hard assets

Hard-asset ABF is growing fastest where digital infrastructure needs capital. Lenders finance the asset and its lease or contract, so residual value and counterparty strength drive losses.

  • Aviation. Aircraft ABS issuance topped US$10bn in 2025, up 85% and the strongest year since 2019; Griffin Global Asset Management priced the largest aircraft ABS on record at US$1.245bn (Maples Group, secondary). Forecasts for 2026 range from US$10bn to US$15bn (GlobalCapital, secondary).
  • Data centers and fiber. Data center ABS and CMBS outstanding grew from US$4bn in 2020 to US$61bn in 2026, and SFA cites projections of US$180bn by 2028 (SFA). The same note puts data center capex through 2028 at US$2.9tn, of which US$1.5tn needs external financing, so securitization can fund only a slice (SFA). KBRA has rated more than US$14bn of fiber ABS from 11 issuers since 2020 without a downgrade (KBRA).
  • GPUs. CoreWeave closed an US$8.5bn non-recourse facility in Mar 2026, the first investment-grade GPU-backed financing, rated A3 by Moody's and priced at SOFR+225bps floating; Blackstone Credit & Insurance anchored it (CoreWeave). Its collateral pairs the GPUs with a contract from an unnamed "leading AI enterprise" (CoreWeave).

6.4 Financial and esoteric assets

Esoteric assets offer diversification, but each market is small and concentrated among a few issuers.

  • Music royalties. KBRA has rated US$12.9bn of music royalty bonds since 2020 and expects issuance to fall about 25% to US$2.5bn in 2026 as catalogs consolidate (Music Business Worldwide, secondary).
  • Litigation finance. US commercial funders committed US$2.8bn in 2025, up 23%, across 39 active funders (Risk & Insurance, secondary, citing Westfleet).
  • Insurance-linked assets. Reinsurance sidecar capital reached a record US$23bn at end-Jun 2026 (Artemis, secondary, citing Aon Securities). Conning projects life settlement volume of about US$4.6bn a year through 2034 (Conning).

7

Structures in depth

Part A · ABF

Section 4 described how each ABF structure works; this section sets out how large each one is, what it costs and where regulators are focusing. Banks remain the senior lenders behind most nonbank ABF, forward flow has become the main funding route for fintech consumer lenders and insurer rating rules shape which structures get built.

7.1 Warehouses and bank lending to nonbanks

Bank lending to nonbanks exceeds US$2tn on the broadest measure, and the credit intermediaries that originate ABF collateral take more than half of it. Three official series measure it differently:

  • Fed H.8: loans to nondepository financial institutions of US$2,066.8bn in the week to Sep 23, 2026, up 11.9% year on year (Federal Reserve H.8).
  • FDIC call reports: US$1.4tn at end-2025, or 5.6% of industry assets, up 35.2% in 2025, partly because of reclassification (FDIC Risk Review 2026).
  • Fed Financial Stability Report: US$2.6tn of bank credit commitments to nonbank financial institutions in Q4 2025, drawn and undrawn (Fed FSR).

A separate FDIC analysis gives the composition, so its shares should not be read against the end-2025 headline. Mortgage credit intermediaries took 24.5% of loans to nondepository financial institutions (NDFIs) at Q3 2025, business credit intermediaries 24.6%, private equity funds 24.0% and consumer credit intermediaries 8.1%; banks with more than US$100bn of assets held about 86% (FDIC).

Filed warehouse terms show tight senior pricing and high advance rates for proven originators. Oportun's four personal loan warehouses total US$1.14bn of capacity at a 95.0% advance rate, priced at Term SOFR+256–318bps with two- to three-year revolving periods (Oportun 10-K via edgar.tools). ACV Auctions' US$125.0mm auto floorplan warehouse pays Term SOFR plus a 0.11448% adjustment plus 3.00%, and its rate can step up after an early amortization event (ACV 10-K). Newer fintech originators pay more: SOFR plus 350–500bps for fintechs with 12–18 months' history, and first-time facilities price higher (Axis Group Ventures, secondary). The FSB puts borrowing-base fund financing at about 225bps (FSB). No citable market-wide data separates senior and mezzanine warehouse pricing.

7.2 Forward flow

Forward flow lets originators grow without holding loans, and the largest single commitment reaches €65bn. The buyer commits to purchase eligible loans as they are made; the seller usually keeps underwriting and servicing, must repurchase ineligible assets and may receive deferred consideration tied to performance (Weil, secondary). A plain forward flow sale generally falls outside the EU and UK Securitisation Regulations, but leverage on the purchased pool can bring it into scope (Weil, secondary). Affirm shows the result at scale: US$28.2bn of committed funding capacity across warehouses, securitizations and forward flow at Mar 31, 2026, with funding costs equal to 1.0% of GMV (Affirm).

7.3 Private ratings and NAIC

Insurers buy much of private ABF in rated form, so NAIC rules on private ratings and capital charges shape structuring. The NAIC told Senator Warren in Sep 2026 that private credit is about 13% of insurer invested assets, with newer forms below 6%, and that private letter ratings are "a meaningful but limited portion" (NAIC). Critics focus on rating quality. Fitch found that 106 of 109 privately rated private credit securities (97%) in 2023 received a higher rating from a rating agency than from the NAIC's Securities Valuation Office (SVO) (Capstone, secondary, citing Fitch). The NAIC says it is "not aware of any material instance" of misclassification (NAIC).

Four rule changes are in motion:

  • Bond definition. The principles-based bond definition took effect on Jan 1, 2025, and Item 2025-29 proposes treating rated notes issued by feeder funds as ABS rather than issuer obligations (Mayer Brown, secondary).
  • Disclosure. From Dec 31, 2026, Item 2025-19 requires insurers to quantify private placements supported only by private letter ratings (FactSet).
  • Capital charges. Collateral loan residual tranches will carry risk-based capital charges scaled by overcollateralization, from 45% below 111% to 22.5% at 200% or more, effective year-end 2027; the 45% charge on CLO residual tranches stays (Sidley, secondary).
  • Rating agency oversight. The NAIC exposed a credit rating provider due diligence framework on May 4, 2026 and is revising it (NAIC).

The stakes are concentrated. The FSB notes that PE-backed insurers control nearly US$900bn of liabilities and have about 27% of their holdings in structured securities, against about 12% at other large insurers (FSB). This FSB measure uses a different source, basis (holdings) and peer group from the NAIC figure used elsewhere in this primer: ABS and other structured securities at 31% of PE-owned insurers' bonds against 13% for the industry at YE2024 (see section 10.3).

7.5 Fund finance and rated feeders

Fund finance is about a US$1.5tn market dominated by bank subscription lines, with NAV loans and rated structures growing fastest. The Fund Finance Association's first survey puts subscription lines alone at US$1.1tn or more and the total at about US$1.5tn, within a US$1.4–1.75tn range; large banks account for about US$800bn of commitments (FFA). Bank loans to private equity funds reached US$308.9bn at Q3 2025 (FDIC).

  • Pricing rises with risk. The FSB puts subscription lines at about 200bps and NAV facilities at 200–400bps (FSB).
  • NAV loans stay modestly levered. Loan-to-value ratios are "generally limited to 30%" (FSB). Oaktree and 17Capital forecast NAV deployment of US$70bn in 2025 and US$145bn in 2030 (Oaktree, interested party).
  • Rated feeders and CFOs package fund exposure for insurers. KBRA rated US$9bn of rated note feeders and US$16bn of collateralized fund obligations by Sep 2025, each above any prior full year, with no downgrades in 2025 (KBRA). Full-year KBRA-rated CFOs reached a record US$26.2bn (Gibson Dunn, secondary, citing KBRA).

A small group of large alternative managers dominates private ABF, and most of them pair third-party funds with an affiliated or partnered insurance balance sheet. Banks have moved from competitors to partners: they source assets, provide senior leverage and sell loan books to the same managers. The three groups now form one funding chain, which is why the FIG sections that follow (sections 9–12) keep returning to ABF.

8.3 The insurer–alternative manager model

Insurance liabilities give managers long-dated capital, and managers give insurers spread from private origination. The FSB estimates that PE-backed insurers control nearly US$900bn of insurance liabilities, up from US$67bn in 2012 (FSB). The NAIC counted 137 PE-owned US insurers at YE2024 with US$704.3bn of cash and invested assets, 7.8% of the industry (NAIC). Section 10 covers the regulatory debate; this section covers how the model works.

Three ownership forms coexist.

  • Owned insurer. Apollo owns Athene, which had US$314bn of net invested assets and a net spread of 1.14% at Q2 2026 (Apollo 8-K via StockTitan). KKR owns Global Atlantic, with US$220bn of AUM, of which US$164bn is credit (KKR Q2 2026 release). Brookfield Wealth Solutions held total assets of US$205.7bn after completing its acquisition of UK pension-risk insurer Just Group (Brookfield Wealth Solutions Q2 2026 release).
  • Minority stake plus advisory mandate. Carlyle cut its Fortitude Re stake to about 10.5% in 2022; the advisory agreement was expected to add US$50bn of fee-earning AUM and US$50mm of annual fee-related earnings (Carlyle).
  • Third-party mandates. Blackstone manages US$290bn of insurance AUM, up 15% y/y, for 40 insurance clients (Blackstone Q2 2026 call, via Investing.com). Insurance SMAs supplied US$7.5bn of its US$9.9bn infrastructure and asset-based credit inflows in Q2 2026 (Blackstone 2Q26 release).

The balance sheets tilt toward structured and asset-backed credit. Athene held 14% of net invested assets in ABS, 14% in residential mortgage loans, 12% in commercial mortgage loans and 7% in CLOs at Jun 30, 2026 (Athene investor presentation, interested party). Across PE-owned insurers, ABS and other structured securities made up 31% of bonds, against 13% for all insurers, with about 96% rated NAIC 1–2 (NAIC).

The spread advantage is the economic engine. Athene says about US$25bn of its Q2 2026 deployment was investment grade credit directly originated by Apollo, at about 190bps over comparably rated public corporates (Athene investor presentation, interested party). Brookfield Wealth Solutions deployed over US$5bn into Brookfield-originated strategies at a target yield of about 7% (Brookfield Wealth Solutions). The manager earns fees on the assets, the insurer earns the spread over its annuity crediting rate, and the capital rarely leaves: perpetual capital is 60% of Apollo's total AUM (Apollo 8-K via StockTitan). Bermuda anchors the model offshore: KBRA says it regulates about one-third of global asset-intensive reinsurance and holds more than 80% of offshore-reinsured US life and annuity liabilities (Royal Gazette on KBRA, secondary).

Banks

Part B · FIG

Banks on both sides of the Atlantic are earning strong returns on solid capital, yet they keep ceding direct corporate and consumer lending to nonbanks and refinancing those nonbanks instead. US banks earn wider margins, while European banks hold more capital and run on thinner margins. The link to ABF runs mainly through warehouse, fund finance and other loans to nondepository financial institutions.

9.1 Fundamentals

US bank profits rose sharply in Q2 2026. FDIC-insured institutions earned US$90.1bn, up 28.7% y/y, for an ROA of 1.37% (KBRA on FDIC QBP). Loans grew 6.8% y/y to US$13.9tn (Premier Insights, secondary). The five largest banks earned a combined US$49bn, up 39% (Quartz, secondary). JPMorgan alone reported US$21.2bn of net income (US$16.9bn excluding significant items) and a 29% ROTCE on US$5.0tn of total assets (JPMorgan 8-K).

The 2022–23 rate shock still sits on US balance sheets. Unrealized securities losses were US$326.7bn at Q2 2026, including US$216.9bn on held-to-maturity securities (Premier Insights, secondary). The Deposit Insurance Fund reached US$161.1bn, a reserve ratio of 1.48% (KBRA).

European banks earn lower returns on higher capital. EU/EEA banks reported CET1 of 16.1%, ROE of 11.3% and an NPL ratio of 1.8% in Q2 2026 (EBA Risk Dashboard). Euro area significant institutions earned an annualised ROE of 10.73% with a NIM of 1.54% (ECB Banking Supervision). Among the largest names, HSBC earned US$19.5bn of H1 2026 pre-tax profit with an 18.2% RoTE and 14.1% CET1 (HSBC).

9.2 Economics: margin, fees and capital

Net interest income drives both systems, but US margins are about twice European ones (author's arithmetic). US banks' NIM was 3.31% in Q1 2026, after an 8bps decline (Premier Insights, secondary), against 1.54% for euro area significant institutions (see Exhibit 16). European banks compensate with cost discipline and fee growth; BNP Paribas grew H1 2026 revenue 10.2% and pre-tax profit 21.3% (TAB Insights, secondary). Partnerships with private credit (section 8.2) let banks earn origination and syndication fees without a full capital charge.

Capital rules decide which assets banks keep. The US Basel III endgame re-proposal of Mar 19, 2026 carries an estimated −4.8% change in CET1 requirements for Category I–II banks (Freshfields, secondary); section 15 covers the detail. It would cut the risk weight on investment grade corporate exposures from 100% to 65% (Fed, Bowman speech). The 2026 Fed stress test projected US$708bn of losses and a 1.6pp aggregate CET1 decline across 32 banks (Federal Reserve). Banks' share of US corporate lending fell from 48% in 2015 to 29% in 2025 (Fed, Bowman speech), and senior lending to nonbanks plus SRT (section 7.4) lets them keep exposure at lower capital cost.

9.3 Lending to nonbanks

Loans to nondepository financial institutions (NDFIs) are growing faster than bank lending overall. Commercial banks held US$2,066.8bn in the week to Sep 23, 2026, up 11.9% y/y (Fed H.8), against 6.8% growth in total loans (see section 9.1). The FDIC call report measure, defined differently, was US$1.4tn at end-2025, 5.6% of industry assets, up 35.2% in the year partly because of reclassification (FDIC Risk Review 2026). Section 7.1 compares the measures; the bank-side view adds three points.

  • Exposure is concentrated in the largest banks. Banks with more than US$100bn of assets held about 86% of NDFI loans at Q3 2025, and ten institutions about 71% (FDIC). At banks above US$250bn, NDFI loans were 15.6% of total loans, against 1.2% in 2010 (PYMNTS on FDIC, secondary).
  • The borrowers are mostly other lenders. Business credit intermediaries took 24.6% of NDFI loans at Q3 2025, mortgage intermediaries 24.5%, private equity funds 24.0% and consumer credit intermediaries 8.1% (FDIC). Much of this is the warehouse and fund finance described in section 4.
  • Undrawn commitments add a contingent layer. The Fed puts bank credit commitments to nonbank financial institutions at US$2.6tn in Q4 2025, with PE, BDC and private credit borrowers about 25% of the total (Fed Financial Stability Report).

Regulators see the lending as safer than direct exposure but harder to monitor. Senior, over-collateralized loans to a nonbank should lose less than the underlying assets, yet the FDIC warns that in a downturn "NDFIs may need to sell assets, causing downward pressure on asset values" (PYMNTS on FDIC, secondary). Fed Vice Chair for Supervision Bowman called the reporting codes "too broad" to separate hedge funds, PE funds, private credit funds, BDCs and SPEs (Fed). The largest recent losses involved alleged collateral fraud: Tricolor cost JPMorgan US$170mm, Fifth Third US$178mm and Barclays US$150mm in Q3 2025 (Banking Dive, secondary; see section 16). Euro area banks' direct private credit exposure is smaller, at €62.5bn or 0.2% of assets (Insurance Journal on ECB, secondary).

Insurers

Part B · FIG

Life insurers have become the largest structural buyers of private credit and ABF, because record annuity sales give them long-dated liabilities that they back with private, often asset-backed, investment-grade paper. US insurers held US$9.6tn of cash and invested assets at end-2025, up 6.7% (NAIC). Life companies held US$6.1tn of that total and property and casualty (P&C) companies US$3.1tn (NAIC).

10.1 Balance sheets and the shift to private assets

Bonds still dominate, but a growing share is private. Bonds made up 59.8% of US insurer assets at end-2025, the first reading below 60%, while Schedule BA assets (the bucket for funds and other alternatives) rose 10.5% to US$639bn (NAIC). Privately placed securities were 48.4% of life-industry bonds, against 37.4% five years earlier (Insurance Business, secondary, reporting NAIC).

Private credit exposure depends on the definition. The NAIC puts insurer private credit at about US$1.2tn, about 13% of cash and invested assets and about 21% of bonds, a broad measure that includes traditional private placements (Insurance Business, secondary). Moody's counts US$807bn of "private and illiquid holdings" for the same date (Insurance Business, secondary). The NAIC told Congress that less than 6% sits in newer structures and that life insurers hold more than US$500bn of traditional private placements (NAIC letter to Sen. Warren). The FSB estimates that life insurers allocate about 10% of portfolios to private credit, against about 3% for non-life insurers (FSB).

10.2 Annuities drive the demand

US retail annuity sales reached a record US$464.1bn in 2025, up 7%, the fourth straight record year (LIMRA). Spread products led: fixed-rate deferred annuities sold US$165.3bn, fixed indexed annuities a record US$127.9bn and registered index-linked annuities a record US$79.5bn, up 20% (LIMRA). Each fixed annuity sold creates a liability that earns the insurer the gap between its portfolio yield and the rate credited to the policyholder, so private assets that add yield at the same rating widen that spread. Athene, for example, reported a net spread of 1.14% in Q2 2026 (Apollo 8-K). The Fed counts US$531bn of life insurer nontraditional liabilities at Q4 2025, up 15% in real terms, and describes life insurer leverage as "well into the upper quartile" of its history (Fed FSR; Fed FSR).

10.3 PE- and manager-affiliated insurers

Alternative managers own or advise a large and fast-growing slice of the life industry. ALIRT counts 93 privately owned, largely PE-linked life insurers with about US$1.2tn of assets, 19.8% of the US life industry, against 16 companies and US$85bn (2.5%) in 2011 (ALIRT via Insurance Business, secondary). The NAIC's narrower PE-owned group held US$704.3bn (7.8% of the industry) at end-2024 (NAIC). The FSB puts liabilities at PE-backed insurers at nearly US$900bn, against US$67bn in 2012 (FSB).

These insurers tilt toward structured credit. ABS and other structured securities made up 31% of PE-owned insurers' bonds, against 13% for the industry, with about 96% rated NAIC 1–2 (NAIC). PE- and manager-led life insurers hold 50% of the industry's affiliated fixed-income investments and 95% of its affiliated ABS (Retirement Income Journal, secondary, reporting NAIC). Athene shows the model at work: about US$25bn of its Q2 2026 deployment was investment-grade credit originated by Apollo at about 190bps over comparably rated public corporates, and 98% of its US$230bn of available-for-sale securities carry NAIC 1 or 2 designations (Athene, interested party). Section 8 covers how managers build these links.

10.4 Bermuda and Europe

Bermuda is the main offshore home for US annuity risk. Bermuda's long-term reinsurance industry held US$1.52tn of assets (Insurance Business, secondary, citing BMA data). KBRA estimates that Bermuda regulates about one-third of global asset-intensive reinsurance and holds more than 80% of offshore-reinsured US life and annuity liabilities (Royal Gazette on KBRA, secondary). The Bermuda Monetary Authority reported a median solvency ratio of 259% for long-term reinsurers at end-2023 and says 77% of their assets are investment grade or higher (BMA). US Treasury and the NAIC are now scrutinizing offshore reinsurance and private letter ratings (see section 15).

European insurers hold far less private credit, and Solvency II is easing securitisation charges. Euro area insurers held €211bn of private credit, 2.3% of assets (ECB via Insurance Journal, secondary). The revised Solvency II framework applies from Jan 30, 2027 and aligns capital factors for senior STS securitisation tranches with covered bonds, with lower factors for non-STS senior and non-senior tranches (European Commission). Allianz, the largest European insurer, reported a Solvency II ratio of 225% at Jun 30, 2026 (Allianz).

Global assets under management (AuM) reached US$147tn at end-2025, but about 80% of the industry's 2025 revenue growth came from market performance and margins sat at about 30% (BCG). 11.2 The wealth channel

Individual investors are the newest source of permanent capital, and the first to test its liquidity. Blackstone's BCRED, a perpetual non-traded BDC, held US$94.6bn and its private equity vehicle BXPE US$25.3bn at Jun 30, 2026 (Blackstone). Retirement plans could be next: an executive order of Aug 7, 2025 led to a Department of Labor proposed rule on Mar 30, 2026 that offers a process-based safe harbor for alternatives in 401(k) plans, which remains a proposal (Kirkland & Ellis, secondary). In Europe, Apollo launched three evergreen ELTIFs under ELTIF 2.0 in Sep 2025 (Apollo).

Redemptions now constrain the channel. BCRED's Q2 2026 repurchase requests exceeded its 5% limit, about 50% were met, and the fund had net outflows of US$1.2bn (Blackstone Q2 call). Section 12 sets out the wider BDC picture.

11.3 Consolidation

Traditional managers are buying private credit capability rather than building it. BlackRock now owns HPS, which managed US$381bn at Mar 31, 2026 (Citi–HPS release). Blue Owl agreed to buy Atalaya in a deal announced on Jul 16, 2024, for US$450mm plus an earnout of up to US$350mm (Blue Owl), and Brookfield, which owns Oaktree, describes an ABF platform of more than US$60bn (Oaktree). Section 8 covers ABF-specific manager scale.

Specialty finance, BDCs and nonbanks

Part B · FIG

Nonbanks now hold half of global financial assets and originate most US mortgages and many consumer loans, but they fund themselves largely with bank lines, securitization and, for BDCs, retail capital that is proving less patient than expected. The FSB measures nonbank financial intermediation (NBFI) at US$256.8tn at end-2024, 51% of global financial assets, growing 9.4% against 4.7% for banks (FSB). Its narrow measure, which counts only activities that may create bank-like risks, was US$76.3tn (FSB). Private credit is a small part of either total at US$1.5–2tn (end-2024 data, FSB May 2026 report), and the FSB warns of "severe limitations in the availability of data" (FSB; FSB).

12.1 Nonbank lenders

Nonbanks lead in mortgages and unsecured consumer credit. Nonbanks produced 65.6% of US residential mortgages in 2025, and the top five lenders held 21.5% of originations (Inside Mortgage Finance). Rocket (429,332 loans) and UWM (422,120) led 2025 HMDA loan counts, with JPMorgan fifth (HousingWire, secondary). Fitch estimates that fintechs originated about half of US unsecured personal loans in 2025, against 21% for banks (Asset Securitization Report, secondary). Nonbank shares in auto and other consumer lending were not found in the sources used.

Their funding is the ABF market. Specialty lenders finance originations through bank warehouses, then sell loans through securitization and forward flow (see section 4). Affirm, for example, had US$28.2bn of committed funding capacity across these channels at Mar 31, 2026 (Affirm). Banks direct about 57% of their loans to nondepository institutions toward credit intermediaries such as mortgage, business and consumer lenders (FDIC via PYMNTS, secondary); section 7 sets out total bank lending to nonbanks. The FDIC warns that in a downturn these borrowers "may need to sell assets, causing downward pressure on asset values" (FDIC via PYMNTS, secondary).

12.2 BDCs

Perpetual non-traded BDCs now hold most BDC assets. The BDC universe reached US$575bn across 179 funds in Q1 2026 (LSTA). Perpetual-life BDCs held US$309.5bn in 53 funds, public BDCs US$176bn and other non-traded and private BDCs US$89bn (LSTA). Perpetual-life funds are 30% of the universe by count but about 54% by assets (author's arithmetic). The Fed puts perpetual-life BDCs at US$306bn gross and US$161bn net assets, and with interval funds at US$425bn gross, at Q4 2025 (Fed FSR).

Credit quality is softening from a low base. The weighted average BDC non-accrual rate rose to 1.99% in Q1 2026, from 1.42% in Q4 2025 and 1.36% a year earlier, with average leverage of 0.92x (LSTA). PIK income fell to 4.33% of new originations from 6.11% the prior quarter (LSTA). Ares Capital, the largest listed BDC, reported a US$29,349mm portfolio across 619 companies, non-accruals of 2.4% at cost and 1.4% at fair value and debt-to-equity of 1.15x at Jun 30, 2026 (Ares Capital). Across private credit more broadly, Fitch's trailing default rate reached a record 6.3% in Aug 2026, and PIK or interest deferrals made up 47% of default events (Benzinga via Webull citing Fitch, secondary).

12.3 Redemption pressure in non-traded BDCs

Non-traded BDCs offer quarterly liquidity against illiquid loans, and in 2026 investors asked for more than the funds would pay. Nine perpetual BDCs tracked by JPMorgan received US$13.4bn of redemption requests in Q1 2026 and met US$7.8bn, after requests of US$7.2bn the prior quarter (LSTA). Blue Owl's technology-focused OTIC received requests equal to 40.7% of shares in Q1 2026 and 38.1% in Q2 against a 5% cap, while its larger OCIC fund received 21.9% and 18.8% (PitchBook; Reuters via WMBD, secondary). Requests at the largest non-traded BDC managers ran at 9–17% in Q2 2026 (Reuters via WMBD, secondary). The IMF notes that "rising borrower stress and growing retail exposure could test semiliquid structures" (IMF). iCapital, an interested party, estimates that requests at two to four times the limit could keep funds oversubscribed for three to five quarters (iCapital). Section 16 treats the episode alongside other liquidity failures.

The money

Economics & Regulation

Returns rise with proximity to the first loss and to the origination franchise. Senior lenders earn a few hundred bps over SOFR, SRT investors target returns from mid-single digits to mid-teens (KKR, see Exhibit 20), and alternative managers turn fees on permanent capital into the highest margins in the sector. Banks earn the lowest return on assets but apply the most leverage.

The originator earns the gross asset yield and passes most of it up the stack. US and UK auto and card loans often price "above 20%" at origination (AllianceBernstein, interested party). Senior warehouse lenders take the safest slice at the thinnest spread: Oportun's warehouse trusts advance 95% at Term SOFR + 2.56% to + 3.18% (Oportun 10-K). Private ABF captures a premium over the public takeout market, with PIMCO putting unlevered private deal spreads at 300–450bps, about 240bps over public ABS (PIMCO, interested party). 13.3 Profit pools

The profit pool follows control of origination and of long-dated capital. Originators that sell assets but keep servicing retain a fee stream: PayPal keeps underwriting and servicing on receivables sold to Blue Owl and KKR (PayPal), and Sallie Mae retains servicing and earns fees under its KKR partnership (Sallie Mae).

Managers earn the most durable fees on perpetual capital. Perpetual capital makes up 60% of Apollo's AUM and 70% of its fee-generating AUM (Apollo 8-K), and 85% of Blue Owl's FRE management fees come from permanent capital (Blue Owl 8-K). KKR holds US$72bn of committed capital, at a weighted fee rate of about 90bps, that is not yet paying fees (KKR).

Insurers capture the spread on private origination. Athene deployed about US$25bn of Apollo-originated investment-grade credit in Q2 2026 at about 190bps over comparably rated public corporates (Athene, interested party). Banks increasingly earn by financing the nonbanks that hold the loans (see section 7.1) and through origination partnerships (see section 8).

Banks have become lenders to their competitors. The bank share of US corporate lending fell from 48% in 2015 to 29% in 2025 (Federal Reserve, Bowman), while bank loans to nonbanks grew fast (see section 7.1 for the H.8 and call report measures). That lending is concentrated: banks with more than US$100bn of assets held about 86% of NDFI loans and ten institutions about 71% (FDIC, Q3 2025). About half of the companies that borrow from private credit funds also borrow from banks (FSB).

A few platforms hold much of dedicated ABF capital. KKR reported US$91bn of ABF AUM (KKR, Q2 2026) and Ares about US$57.3bn (Ares, Mar 31, 2026). Together they equal about one-third of Blue Owl's US$450bn estimate of dedicated ABF manager AUM (author's arithmetic, mixed dates; PitchBook). SRT is more concentrated still: the largest single investor holds 31% of European exposures (IMF).

Four barriers protect incumbents.

  • Origination. Apollo sourced US$127bn of its US$309bn 2025 origination through 16 proprietary platforms (Apollo, interested party); KKR owns 18 captive ABF platforms (KKR, Jul 2025).
  • Servicing. Sellers usually keep servicing, so buyers depend on the originator's operations (see section 4.4).
  • Data and controls. The alleged 2025–26 fraud cases show the cost of relying on borrower-supplied collateral data (see section 16).
  • Long-dated capital. Blackstone manages US$290bn for insurers (Blackstone call), and KKR's Global Atlantic holds US$164bn of credit AUM (KKR); see section 10.

Substitutes cap private pricing. Public securitization absorbs the most standard assets: about US$339bn of US ABS in 2025 (Valuation Research, secondary) and €252.3bn of European issuance (AFME). Banks can also sell whole books, as HSBC did with A$36bn of Australian loans to Blackstone (FinanceAsia, secondary), buy protection through SRT, or co-originate through partnerships such as Citi–HPS (€15bn) (Citi–HPS release).

15

Regulation and policy

Economics & Regulation

Policy is moving in two directions. Bank capital rules are easing in the US and being phased in with relief in Europe, while scrutiny is moving to where the risk now sits: insurer balance sheets, bank lines to nonbanks and semi-liquid retail funds.

15.1 Banks and securitization

US agencies re-proposed the Basel III endgame on Mar 19, 2026, together with a revised standardized approach and a GSIB surcharge re-proposal. The endgame framework would be mandatory only for Category I–II banks, and Category III–IV banks may opt in. Across the package, estimated CET1 changes are −4.8% for Category I–II banks, −5.2% for Category III–IV and −7.8% for smaller banks (Freshfields, secondary). The re-proposal would cut the risk weight on investment-grade corporates from 100% to 65% (Federal Reserve, Bowman). The 2026 stress test cut aggregate CET1 by 1.6pp across 32 banks, on losses of US$708bn (Federal Reserve).

Europe is changing the securitization rules that shape bank demand for SRT and ABS. The European Parliament's position cuts the senior non-STS p-factor from 1.0 to 0.6 (Orrick, interested party), but the third trilogue on Sep 29, 2026 ended without agreement (Agence Europe, secondary). Supervisors are cautious on SRT. The Basel Committee flagged bank financing of protection sellers and capital relief that exceeds real risk transfer (Jones Day, secondary). Even so, the ECB now approves qualifying SRT deals in two weeks (ECB); section 7.4 covers the mechanics.

15.2 Insurers

US regulators are tightening how insurers capitalize and rate private assets. The NAIC will require insurers to quantify placements supported only by private letter ratings from year-end 2026 (FactSet) and will replace the flat 6.8% charge on collateral loans with charges of 22.5–45% on residual tranches, scaled by overcollateralization, from year-end 2027 (Sidley, secondary). Fitch found that 106 of 109 privately rated private credit securities rated in 2023 received a higher rating than the SVO's original assessment (Capstone, secondary, citing Fitch). The NAIC says it is "not aware of any material instance" of misclassification (NAIC); section 7.3 covers private ratings. The US Treasury has joined scrutiny of Bermuda reinsurance (Insurance Business, secondary). In Europe, the revised Solvency II applies from Jan 30, 2027 and aligns STS senior securitization charges with covered bonds (European Commission).

15.3 Retail access and system monitoring

US policy is widening retail access just as official monitors question semi-liquid structures. The Department of Labor proposed a safe harbor for alternatives in 401(k) plans on Mar 30, 2026 (Kirkland & Ellis, secondary). The IMF warns that "rising borrower stress and growing retail exposure could test semiliquid structures" (IMF GFSR). The FSB's May 2026 report lists leverage, liquidity mismatch, bank and insurer interlinkages, retail access and data gaps as the main private credit vulnerabilities (FSB). The Bank of England is running a private markets stress exercise with 46 participants; final results are due in early 2027 (Bank of England).

16

What goes wrong

Economics & Regulation

The largest recent ABF losses involved collateral that was allegedly missing or pledged twice, not weak asset performance. Earlier FIG failures came from funding runs and concentrated counterparty exposure. Today's live risks combine both: rising redemption requests at semi-liquid funds and a corporate private credit default rate at a record.

16.1 Collateral that was not there

Three cases in twelve months showed how warehouse and receivables structures fail when lenders rely on borrower-supplied data. First Brands carried debt of "over US$11bn," including US$4.6bn off balance sheet, and some invoices were reportedly financed more than once (BRAKE Report, secondary; Willkie, secondary); the case converted to Chapter 7 on Aug 24, 2026 (BRAKE Report, secondary). Tricolor allegedly pledged the same auto loans to several warehouse lines and kept pledging them after sale into securitizations (Banking Dive, secondary). Its three disclosed bank losses total US$498mm (author's arithmetic). Market Financial Solutions, a UK bridging lender, entered administration in Feb 2026 with an estimated shortfall to creditors of more than £1.3bn (Bridging & Commercial, secondary). Lawyers now recommend lockbox accounts, fresh lien searches, independent receivable verification and never letting the borrower act as sole servicer (Willkie, secondary).

16.2 Runs and gates

Liquidity mismatch is the risk that moved from banks to funds. In 2023, SVB faced US$42bn of withdrawal requests in one day (Wikipedia, secondary). In 2026, the pressure fell on non-traded BDCs. Blue Owl's OTIC received requests for 40.7% of shares in Q1 2026 against a 5% cap (PitchBook), so holders received about one-eighth of what they asked for (author's arithmetic). Across nine perpetual BDCs, requests of US$13.4bn met US$7.8bn of payouts in Q1 2026 (LSTA). Section 12 covers BDC structure.

16.3 Credit and policy shocks

Credit stress is concentrated in corporate private credit and nonprime consumer loans. Fitch's US private credit default rate reached 6.3% in the twelve months to Aug 2026, with PIK and interest deferrals making up 47% of default events (Webull/Benzinga, citing Fitch, secondary). Subprime auto ABS 60+ day delinquency reached 6.74% in Dec 2025 (Auto Finance News, secondary, citing Fitch). Policy can also break a niche: Sunnova and Mosaic filed in Jun 2025 amid high rates and threatened cuts to residential solar tax credits (Solar Builder, secondary).

16.4 Systemic warnings

Official monitors see the main channel as bank exposure to nonbanks. In the IMF's adverse scenario, about 18% of global banks by assets would breach CET1 thresholds, rising to 21% once nonbank shocks are added (IMF GFSR, Oct 2025). The FDIC warns that NDFIs "may need to sell assets" in a downturn, pushing down asset values (PYMNTS, secondary, citing FDIC). The Fed reports life insurer leverage "well into the upper quartile" (Federal Reserve FSR), and the BIS notes that 40–60% repo haircuts cap leverage in the SRT market at about €10bn (BIS).

Not every authority sees a systemic threat. The ECB finds euro area bank exposure to private credit at €62.5bn, or 0.2% of assets (Insurance Journal, secondary, citing ECB), and the OFR estimates a 12-month default probability of 1.3% on bank loans to private credit (OFR). JPMorgan's Jamie Dimon framed the other view after Tricolor: "When you see one cockroach, there are probably more" (Fortune, secondary).

Rule of thumb (author's): in ABF, check whether the collateral exists and is pledged only once before modeling how it performs.

18

Trends and outlook

Players & Outlook

Six trends stand out in late 2026: banks financing nonbanks, insurance capital as anchor, a rising ABF share of fundraising, new collateral, regulatory catch-up and diverging consumer credit.

18.1 Six trends

Banks finance nonbanks rather than compete with them. US bank credit commitments to nonbank financial institutions reached US$2.6tn in Q4 2025; commitments to private equity, BDCs and private credit, about a quarter of the total, grew 17% in 2025 (Fed FSR). Partnerships and portfolio sales extend the pattern, from Citi's €15bn program with HPS to HSBC's A$36bn Australian loan sale to Blackstone (Citi–HPS; FinanceAsia, secondary).

Insurance balance sheets anchor demand. Blackstone's insurance AUM rose 15% year on year to US$290bn in Q2 2026, and US$7.5bn of its US$9.9bn infrastructure and asset-based credit inflows came through insurance SMAs (Blackstone). Brookfield Wealth Solutions raised US$45bn in the quarter, including a US$40bn Just Group mandate (Brookfield Asset Management).

ABF takes a larger share of private credit fundraising. Closed-end ABF rose from 10.6% to 16.4% of closed-end private credit fundraising between 2024 and 2025 (see section 5). Direct lending's share fell from 58.4% in 2024 to about 31% in Q1 2026 (Squire Patton Boggs citing PitchBook, secondary).

Digital infrastructure and home equity add collateral. Data center ABS and CMBS outstanding grew from US$4bn in 2020 to US$61bn in 2026, and the SFA cites projections of US$180bn by 2028 (SFA). CoreWeave closed the first investment-grade GPU-backed financing in Mar 2026 (CoreWeave). Second-lien RMBS issuance, US$30bn in 2025, is expected to reach about US$40bn in 2026 (SFA).

Regulation is catching up with the structures. The EU securitisation reform, the US Basel III endgame re-proposal and NAIC capital and disclosure changes all reach decision points in the next six months (see section 15 and Exhibit 33). The Bank of England's private markets stress exercise, with 46 participants, reports in early 2027 (Bank of England; Bank of England scenario).

Consumer credit is splitting by borrower quality. KBRA's non-prime auto ABS annualized net loss rose 61bps year on year to 9.46% in Jul 2026, while the prime index held at 0.66% (KBRA). Bank credit card charge-offs fell to 3.82% in Q2 2026 from 4.19% a year earlier (Federal Reserve via FRED).

18.2 Outlook

Published forecasts point to continued growth, although most come from interested parties. KBRA expects US ABS issuance of US$385.2bn in 2026, about 5% above KBRA's own 2025 estimate (KBRA). Moody's expects private credit AUM to exceed US$2tn in 2026 and approach US$4tn by 2030 (Moody's). McKinsey estimates that US$5–6tn of US lending could shift to nonbanks over the next decade (McKinsey). KKR's projection of a US$9tn ABF market by 2029 is discussed in section 3.

Three factors could slow that path. New fraud cases would raise diligence costs and bank caution (see section 16). Redemption requests above quarterly caps would constrain the wealth channel; nine perpetual BDCs received US$13.4bn of requests in Q1 2026 and fulfilled US$7.8bn (LSTA). Tighter insurer capital rules would raise the cost of the largest buyer base. Looser bank capital rules point the other way: the Basel re-proposal would cut the risk weight on investment-grade corporate exposures from 100% to 65%, lowering banks' cost of holding such loans themselves (Federal Reserve).

Glossary

Appendices

Terms defined in Exhibit 3 are not repeated here.

Annualized net loss (ANL)
A pool's net credit losses over a period, scaled to a yearly rate; KBRA's auto and solar indices use it (see section 6).
Back-up servicer
A party contracted to take over collections if the primary servicer fails; arrangements are graded cold, warm or hot by readiness (see section 4.4).
Basel III endgame
The US implementation of the final Basel III bank capital standards; Basel 3.1 is the UK equivalent (see section 15).
Business development company (BDC)
A US closed-end vehicle that lends mainly to middle-market companies; it may be listed, privately offered or perpetual non-traded (see section 12.2).
Buy now, pay later (BNPL)
Short-term installment credit offered at the point of sale, funded largely through forward flow (see section 6.1).
Closed-end second and HELOC
Second-lien mortgage loans: a fixed loan or a revolving home equity line behind a first mortgage (see section 6.1).
Collateralized loan obligation (CLO)
A securitization of leveraged corporate loans, tranched from AAA notes to a residual equity tranche.
Credit-linked note (CLN)
A note whose repayment falls if losses hit a reference pool; banks issue CLNs to fund SRT protection (see section 7.4).
Early amortization
A trigger that stops a facility's revolving period and diverts collections to repay senior lenders, often with higher pricing (see section 4.2).
ELTIF
European long-term investment fund, the EU wrapper used to sell private-market funds to individual investors (see section 11.2).
eSLR
The enhanced supplementary leverage ratio applied to US global systemically important banks (see section 15).
FRTB
Fundamental Review of the Trading Book, the Basel standard for market-risk capital (see section 15).
Interval fund
A US registered fund that offers to repurchase a set share of its shares at regular intervals, usually quarterly (see section 11.2).
Lockbox (controlled collection account)
An account that receives borrower payments under lender control rather than the originator's (see section 16.1).
Nondepository financial institution (NDFI)
The bank reporting category for loans to nonbank lenders, funds and special-purpose entities (see sections 7.1 and 9.3).
Nonbank financial intermediation (NBFI)
The FSB's term for credit and investment activity outside banks; its narrow measure counts only bank-like risks (see section 12).
Non-accrual
A loan on which the lender has stopped recognizing interest income because payment is in doubt.
p-factor
The supervisory parameter in the EU securitization capital formula that sets how much capital a tranche carries relative to the underlying pool (see section 15.1).
Payment in kind (PIK)
Interest paid by adding to the loan balance rather than in cash.
Perpetual capital
Manager AUM with no fixed end date, such as insurance mandates and perpetual BDCs, which pays fees indefinitely (see section 11.1).
Private letter rating
A credit rating issued to the investor or issuer but not published; US insurers use them to obtain NAIC designations (see section 7.3).
Residual tranche
The most junior piece of a securitization, which receives what is left after all notes are paid.
Return on risk-adjusted capital (RoRAC)
Profit divided by the capital a position requires; SRT raises it by cutting required capital (see section 7.4).
Schedule BA
The US insurer statutory schedule for funds, joint ventures and other alternative assets (see section 10.1).
Securities Valuation Office (SVO)
The NAIC unit that assigns and reviews NAIC designations (see section 7.3).
Separately managed account (SMA)
A mandate run for a single investor, such as an insurer, rather than a pooled fund.
Servicer
The party that collects payments, manages delinquencies and reports pool data; in ABF it is usually the originator (see section 4.4).
Solvency II
The EU prudential regime for insurers, which sets capital charges by asset type (see sections 10.4 and 15.2).
Special-purpose vehicle (SPV)
A bankruptcy-remote entity that holds the asset pool and borrows against it (see section 4.2).
Spread-related earnings (SRE)
An alternative manager's earnings from the investment spread of an owned insurer (see section 11.1).
STS securitisation
A simple, transparent and standardized EU securitization that qualifies for lower capital charges (see sections 10.4 and 15).
Whole-loan sale
The outright sale of an existing loan book in one trade (see section 4.3).
